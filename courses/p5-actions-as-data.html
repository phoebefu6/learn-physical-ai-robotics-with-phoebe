Actions as data

In imitation learning the action column is the label, and it is not a given. Someone chose its space (joints or the gripper's position), its reference (an absolute target or a change from where the arm is), its scale (normalised with which statistics) and its length (one step or a chunk of many). Each choice changes what the policy can learn and how its mistakes add up. Tonight you take the column apart and put it back together. Wrenfield Lab and its trajectories are constructed; the research numbers are real and cited.

Part 0

Wrenfield's arm keeps arriving a little to the left

Wrenfield Lab (constructed) trained its first parcel-pick policy on actions stored as small changes per step. In short tests it looked fine. On long picks the gripper ended up centimetres from the parcel, always on the same side. Nothing was broken in the cameras or the calibration. The error was in the action column itself: a tiny bias per step, added up 500 times. Tonight is about the four choices hidden in that column and how each one shows up in the data.

LiveWhy the action column deserves a session3 min▶

In most tabular work the label is fixed by the business: churned or not, price paid. In robot learning the label is a stream of commands, and the team decides how to write it down. Two teams can record the same physical motion and ship datasets that a model sees as completely different, or that a loader silently mixes as if they were the same.

Real world

Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) recorded at 50 Hz and took as the action the joint positions of the leader arms that a person moved, not the robot's own follower arms. At every step the action is the target joint positions for both arms at the next step, and a low-level controller tracks them. That one choice, absolute targets in joint space, is the starting point for everything tonight.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Joint space. The action is one number per motor: an angle or a position for each joint, plus the gripper.
  • End-effector space. The action is where the gripper should be: position and orientation in some frame (practitioner session 2).
  • Absolute target. "Go to this position." Meaningful on its own.
  • Delta, or relative action. "Move by this much from where you are." Meaningful only with the current state.
  • Normalisation statistics. The mean and spread used to rescale actions before training, and to undo the rescaling at run time.
  • Action chunk. A block of k future actions predicted at once.
Part 1 · covers joint vs end-effector, absolute vs delta, position vs velocity

One motion, four ways to write it down

8 min live

Take a tiny motion: the arm moves for two steps, shoulder opening a little, elbow closing a little, the gripper drifting forward and down. You can record the same two steps as joint angles or as gripper positions, and either as where to go or as how far to move. All four are correct. They are not interchangeable, and each one needs something else stored next to it to mean anything.

LiveJoint or end-effector: what each one carries4 min▶

A joint-space action talks directly to the motors. It needs no frame and no calibration, but it only makes sense for one arm design: the same six numbers on a different arm produce a different motion. An end-effector action says where the gripper should go, which is easier to compare across robots, but it lives in a coordinate frame, so it inherits every calibration question from practitioner session 2, and something has to convert it into motor commands.

Real world

In the rig of Zhao et al. (2023), an action is the target joint positions for both arms at the next step, read from the leader arms a person moves, and a low-level controller makes the follower arms track them. The authors chose the leader's positions on purpose: the force applied is implicit in the gap between leader and follower. A joint-space absolute target was the most direct record of what the person did.

LiveAbsolute or delta: what each one assumes2 min▶

An absolute target says "be here next". If one step is wrong, the next step's target is still correct, so errors do not pile up. A delta says "move this much". It is smaller, often more similar across the workspace, and does not care where the arm started, which is why teams like it. But a delta is only meaningful together with the state it was applied to, and every per-step error carries into every later step. Part 2 shows how fast that adds up.

Self-studyPosition or velocity3 min▶

A third axis: the action can be a position (where) or a velocity (how fast). A velocity command held for one control period is a delta in disguise, so it shares the delta's drift. It also ties the action to the clock: the same velocity at 50 Hz and at 10 Hz moves the arm by different amounts per step. Store the control rate in the episode's metadata (practitioner session 1), and resample with care; resampling streams is taught in learn IoT data.

Part 2 · covers drift and normalisation statistics

Deltas drift, absolutes do not

8 min live

Give a policy a tiny, constant bias: every step it is off by the same fraction of a millimetre in the same direction. With absolute targets, each step aims at the right place plus the bias, so the error stays the size of the bias. With deltas, each step moves from where the last one ended, so the bias is added again every step. Ten seconds at 50 Hz is 500 steps. The calculator below does that sum.

Constructed policy and bias; a real policy's errors are not constant and partly cancel, so your numbers will differ. Re-observation here means the policy sees where the arm really is and the error resets, except for the bias of the step it then takes.
LiveThree runs worth doing in the calculator3 min▶
  • The default. 0.2 mm for 500 steps prints 100 mm for deltas and 0.2 mm for absolute targets. A bias you could never see in a single step is a missed parcel by the end.
  • Make the pick longer. Delta drift grows with the number of steps; absolute error does not move. Long tasks punish deltas most.
  • Turn re-observation on. Closing the loop caps the drift at roughly the bias times the re-observation interval. That is why delta policies are run closed loop, and why the interval matters as much as the bias.
LiveNormalisation: three rules for the statistics4 min▶

Actions are rescaled before training so that every dimension has a similar spread: a wrist joint that moves 0.01 rad per step and a gripper that opens 0.05 m should not differ by a factor the optimiser has to discover. Three rules keep this honest.

  • Compute the statistics on the training split only. Statistics from all the data leak the held-out episodes into the model, so the evaluation is no longer held out.
  • Store them with the model. At run time the policy outputs normalised numbers. Without the exact mean and spread used in training, they cannot be turned back into commands.
  • Apply them per dataset. A delta dataset and an absolute dataset have completely different statistics; one shared set squashes one of them into noise.
Real world

When the Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) pooled 60 datasets from 22 robot types, it mapped every dataset to a 7-dimensional end-effector action and normalised each dataset's actions before training, then de-normalised per robot. Its authors still warn that actions may be absolute or relative positions or velocities, and that "the same action vector may induce very different motions for different robots." Practitioner session 4 covers what mixing did to results.

Self-studyWhere normalisation goes wrong quietly3 min▶

Three failures that produce no error message. A gripper dimension that never moves in the training split has a spread of zero, and dividing by it produces infinities: add a small floor. A retrained model ships with the old statistics file: every command is rescaled wrongly by a fixed factor. And a dataset gains a new operator with a different style: the training statistics no longer describe the actions being produced. Check the held-out split's normalised mean and spread every time; the build-along prints both.

Part 3 · covers action chunks and temporal ensembling

Predict a chunk, not a step

7 min live

A policy that outputs one action per step makes 500 decisions in a 10-second pick, and every small mistake can push it into a state the demonstrations never showed. Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) predicts k future actions at once. The arm executes them, then the policy decides again. Fewer decisions, fewer chances for errors to compound. The price is that the arm reacts less often to what it sees.

LiveWhat the chunking ablation found3 min▶
Real world

Zhao et al. (2023) varied the chunk size k with temporal ensembling switched off. Success, averaged over four simulated settings, went from 1 percent at k = 1 to 44 percent at k = 100. Longer still, at k = 200 and k = 400, success dipped slightly: by then the policy is close to running open loop. Switching temporal ensembling on added 3.3 percent for ACT and 4 percent for a baseline method. Chunking also helped two baseline methods, not only ACT.

Read it as a data decision, not a model trick. The label went from "the next action" to "the next k actions". The same recorded episodes became a different dataset, and that change alone moved the success rate by more than forty points in simulation.

LiveWhy chunks help, and what they cost3 min▶
  • Fewer decisions compound. The paper's hypothesis: with k = 100 a 500-step episode needs 5 decisions, not 500, so small errors have fewer chances to push the arm into states the demonstrations never covered.
  • Human pauses stop confusing the model. People hesitate mid-motion; a chunk can represent the pause as part of a plan rather than as an isolated, unexplained step.
  • The trade: less reactive. Inside a chunk the arm executes a plan made earlier. If the parcel slips at step 20 of a 100-step chunk, a policy without ensembling will not see it until step 100. The dip at k = 200 and 400 is that cost showing up.
  • Ensembling buys some reactivity back. Querying every step and averaging overlaps gives a fresh opinion each step while keeping smooth motion, at the price of running the model every step.
Self-studyChunks meet frames and mixed robots3 min▶

A chunk inherits every choice from Parts 1 and 2. A chunk of absolute joint targets is a short trajectory for one arm design. A chunk of deltas sums its own errors across k steps before the policy looks again. A chunk of end-effector targets is only as good as the frame it is written in (practitioner session 2). And when datasets from several robots are mixed (practitioner session 4), the chunk length and the control rate must agree, or the same k means a different number of seconds on each robot.

Build-along

The round trip in numpy: raw, delta, normalise, chunk, back

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Python 3.10 or later with numpy. You build one constructed Wrenfield trajectory, 500 steps of a 7-joint arm, then do what a training loader does: turn absolute targets into deltas, fit normalisation statistics on a training split only, cut the result into overlapping chunks, and reverse every step. If the round trip does not come back to the start, something in the pipeline is lying.

Build the constructed trajectory

A smooth swing on seven joints plus a slow random wander, 500 steps at 50 Hz. Keep the starting state: deltas mean nothing without it.

actions.py, part 1import numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(5) # constructed Wrenfield trajectory hz, steps, joints = 50, 500, 7 t = np.arange(steps) / hz # 10 s at 50 Hz q = (0.4 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.2 * t)[:, None] * np.linspace(0.5, 1.5, joints) + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.002, (steps, joints)), axis=0)) q_start = q[0].copy() # the state before the first action

Absolute targets to deltas

Difference along time. Prepending the first row makes the first delta zero, so the shape stays (500, 7) and step 0 means "stay where you start".

actions.py, part 2# 1. absolute targets -> deltas (relative actions) d = np.diff(q, axis=0, prepend=q[:1]) # d[0] is zero: no move yet print("absolute shape:", q.shape, " delta shape:", d.shape)

Normalise with training statistics only

The first 400 steps are the training split, the last 100 are held out. Fit the mean and spread on the first 400, apply them to everything, and save them next to the model. The held-out split will not come out at exactly 0 and 1, and it should not.

actions.py, part 3# 2. normalisation statistics from the TRAIN split only train, held = d[:400], d[400:] mu, sd = train.mean(axis=0), train.std(axis=0) + 1e-8 z = (d - mu) / sd print("train z mean %.3f std %.3f" % (z[:400].mean(), z[:400].std())) print("held z mean %.3f std %.3f" % (z[400:].mean(), z[400:].std())) np.savez("norm_stats.npz", mu=mu, sd=sd) # ships with the model

Cut into overlapping chunks of k = 50

A stride of 25 means every step after the first 25 sits in two chunks, the same overlap that temporal ensembling averages at run time.

actions.py, part 4# 3. chunks of k = 50, stride 25 (each step lands in up to two chunks) k, stride = 50, 25 starts = range(0, steps - k + 1, stride) chunks = np.stack([z[s:s + k] for s in starts]) print("chunks:", chunks.shape)

Go back, and check the error

Average the overlapping chunk values, undo the normalisation with the saved statistics, and add the deltas up from the starting state.

actions.py, part 5# 4. back: average overlapping chunks, de-normalise, cumulative sum acc = np.zeros_like(z); cnt = np.zeros((steps, 1)) for s, c in zip(starts, chunks): acc[s:s + k] += c; cnt[s:s + k] += 1 d_back = (acc / cnt) * sd + mu q_back = q_start + np.cumsum(d_back, axis=0) print("round-trip max error (rad): %.2e" % np.abs(q_back - q).max())

Run it and read the output

Run python3 actions.py from a scratch folder. This is what the script printed when this page was written; the last line may differ in its tiny digits on another machine, but it should stay near 1e-16.

Expected outputabsolute shape: (500, 7) delta shape: (500, 7) train z mean 0.000 std 1.000 held z mean 0.361 std 0.847 chunks: (19, 50, 7) round-trip max error (rad): 1.12e-16

Extend it: break it on purpose

Replace q_start with q_start + 0.1 and rerun: every reconstructed angle is now off by 0.1 rad, because deltas carry no memory of where they began. Then add 0.0001 to every row of d_back: the error grows with the step count until it reaches 0.05 rad at step 500, which is 0.0001 x 500, the drift from Part 2 in your own numbers.

The habit to keep. Every transform in an action pipeline gets an inverse and a round-trip test. The held-out line, 0.361 and 0.847 rather than 0 and 1, is the sign that the statistics really came from the training split alone.

Check yourself

Three questions

A constructed policy is off by 0.2 mm in the same direction at every step of a 500-step pick. Which action type ends the pick about 100 mm from the target?

Absolute targets stay off by the bias, 0.2 mm, because each step aims at the true position plus the bias. Deltas accumulate: 0.2 mm x 500 = 100 mm. Re-observing the arm's real position bounds the drift.

Where should action normalisation statistics come from?

Statistics from all the data leak the held-out split into training. Without the stored statistics the policy's outputs cannot be turned back into commands. A shared set across delta and absolute datasets squashes one of them.

In the chunking ablation of Zhao et al. (2023), what do k = 1 and a very large k mean?

k = 1 is the classic one-action-per-step policy: many decisions, many chances to compound errors. A very large k executes a long plan without looking, close to open loop. The middle won in that study.

Cheat sheet

The label. In imitation learning the action column is the label, and its format is a design choice.
Four choices. Joint or end-effector, absolute or delta, position or velocity, one step or a chunk.
Context to store. Deltas need the state; end-effector actions need the frame; velocities need the rate.
Drift. Delta error grows as bias x steps; absolute error stays at the bias.
Closing the loop. Re-observing every k steps caps delta drift near bias x k.
Normalisation. Train split only, saved with the model, per dataset, with a floor on the spread.

Round trip. Every transform gets an inverse and a test that comes back to zero.

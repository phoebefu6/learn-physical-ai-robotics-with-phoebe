Shared space and stop logs

When people work in the same cell as the arm, a failure is no longer only a dropped parcel. The robot's safety layer stops or limits the arm, and every one of those stops lands in a log with a time and a cause. Tonight you treat that log as a dataset: what a stop record must contain, which denominator turns a count into a rate that means something, and why a policy that succeeds more is not better by default if it stops more near people. Wrenfield Lab, its two cells and its stop log are constructed; the standards are cited as reported, and nothing here is legal or compliance advice.

Part 0

Wrenfield opens the cell to the packing team

Wrenfield Lab (constructed) has moved its two-arm picker out of the fenced test bench and into a cell where people restock totes and clear jams by hand. In the first week the arm stopped 40 times. The operations lead asks the data team one question: is that a lot? Tonight is about why that question has no answer until you say "per what", and what the log has to record before anyone can answer it honestly.

LiveWhy "40 stops" is not yet a fact3 min▶

A count of stops mixes three things: how risky the policy is, how much work the robot did, and how often people came close. Double the picks and the count roughly doubles with a perfectly unchanged policy. Halve the restocking visits and the count of zone-entry stops drops with the same policy again. A stop count is a numerator waiting for an exposure, and choosing the exposure is a decision about which question you are answering.

Real world

Kress-Gazit et al. (2024), writing on how robot learning should report evaluations, found that papers usually report a success rate with little on the number of runs, the initial conditions, the success criteria or the statistics, and recommended reporting conditions, complementary metrics and qualitative failure modes alongside it. Stop logs are the complementary metric that matters most once people share the space, and they have the same reporting problem: a number with no conditions and no denominator.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Protective stop. The safety layer halts the arm because a monitored condition was met. It is an event in the log with a time and a cause.
  • Force-limit trip. A protective stop because measured contact force passed the configured limit.
  • Zone entry. A stop or slow-down because a person was detected inside a monitored zone.
  • Operator e-stop. A person pressed the emergency stop. A human judgement, like a takeover in practitioner session 8.
  • Exposure. The denominator: hours of shared operation, picks, or person entries into the cell.
  • Near-miss. A situation that came close to a stop or a contact and did not trigger one. Not logged unless someone decides to log it.
Part 1 · covers the cost asymmetry and the safety layer

Near people, being wrong costs something different

9 min live

On the fenced bench, a wrong action costs a missed grasp: one failed trial, a reset, a line in the evaluation. In a shared cell the same wrong action can move the arm toward a person. The two errors are not on the same scale, so they cannot share one metric. The policy is judged on success; the cell is judged on stops, and the safety layer that produces those stops sits underneath the policy, not inside it.

LiveWhat the standards say, as reported4 min▶

Nothing on this page is legal or compliance advice. Whether a cell is safe is decided by a risk assessment against the standards, done by qualified people; a data team does not certify anything. What a data team needs is to know the shape of the rules, because the rules decide what gets logged.

Real world

ISO/TS 15066:2016 (reported; the standard is paywalled and was not read at source) describes power and force limiting as one of four collaborative operation modes, and its Annex A tabulates maximum contact force and pressure per body region, set from pain-onset thresholds and described as preliminary. Behrens et al. (2022), in Frontiers in Robotics and AI (reported), ran a study with 112 subjects toward replacing those values and found that pain thresholds differ between men and women in some body regions. ISO 10218-1 and -2 were revised in 2025 (reported), consolidating the collaborative content into Part 1. No per-region value is quoted here, deliberately.

The data consequence: the limit that trips a contact stop is a configured number, and the science behind it is still moving. A force-limit trip count is only comparable across weeks if the limit was the same in both weeks. So the configured limit, or its version, belongs in the stop record.

LiveThe safety layer sits under the policy3 min▶

The learned policy proposes actions; the safety layer can veto, slow or stop them, whatever the policy wanted. That separation is why stops are such clean data: they are written by a component the policy cannot talk its way past. It also means a stop says "the safety layer intervened", not "the policy was about to hurt someone". A zone-entry stop fires the same way whether the arm was heading toward the person or away. Read stops as exposure to risk, not as a count of averted accidents.

Self-studyAsymmetric costs, in data terms3 min▶

When two kinds of error have very different costs, averaging them into one score hides the expensive one. Keep two ledgers: task outcomes (success, failure, intervention) and safety events (stops by cause). Report them side by side, never as a single blended number. The same reasoning appears in any domain with a rare, costly error; the statistics of very rare events over very long exposure are taught in learn autonomous driving.

Part 2 · covers the stop record and its denominators

A stop log is a dataset with three denominators

8 min live

A protective stop record is a row with a time, a cause, the robot's state at the moment, the policy version that was running, the configured limit, and whether a person was present. With those fields the log can answer questions. Without a denominator it still cannot answer "is that a lot". Wrenfield's two cells, both constructed, show why the choice of denominator is the choice of question.

Cell B (a hand-off station people visit often and briefly): 30 stops (15 zone entry, 9 contact force limit, 6 operator e-stop), 2,500 picks, 30 shared hours, 600 person entries. Constructed data, computed in this page with the engine's Wilson score interval (PAI.wilson). The per-pick and per-entry intervals treat each pick or entry as having at most one stop, which holds for this constructed log. A per-hour rate is a count over time and needs a Poisson interval, not Wilson, so none is printed for it.
LiveRead the default, then switch4 min▶

At the default, all causes per 1,000 picks, the widget prints 6.67 for Cell A (95% interval 4.90 to 9.06) and 12.00 for Cell B (8.42 to 17.08). Cell A looks better, but the intervals overlap, so one week cannot separate them on this measure. Switch to per 100 person entries: Cell A prints 13.33 (9.95 to 17.65) and Cell B 5.00 (3.52 to 7.05). Now the ranking has flipped, and the intervals no longer overlap.

Neither number is wrong. Per pick answers "how often does the work get interrupted". Per person entry answers "when someone walks in, how likely is a stop". Cell B is visited twice as often for a fraction of the work, so it looks worse per pick and better per visit. Pick the denominator from the question before you look at the numbers, and write it into the report header, because the reader will otherwise assume the one that flatters their prior.

LiveWhat a stop record must contain3 min▶
  • Time, on the same clock as the episode (practitioner session 1), so the stop joins to what the robot was doing.
  • Cause: contact force limit, zone entry, operator e-stop, or another category the safety layer reports. One field, a fixed vocabulary.
  • Robot state: moving, grasping, placing; joint positions if available.
  • Policy version, so a new policy's stops are never mixed with the old one's.
  • Configured limit or zone version, because the limit can change and the count changes with it.
  • Person present: yes or no, and from which sensor.

And outside the record, the exposure for the same period: shared hours, picks and person entries, logged by the same system so the denominators cover exactly the stops' time window.

Self-studyThe same shape as the intervention log2 min▶

Operator e-stops are human judgements, like the takeovers in practitioner session 8: their rate depends on the person watching as much as on the robot. Report them as their own cause, never folded into the automatic stops. Zone-entry and force-limit stops are written by sensors and a configured rule; their rate depends on the rule's version and on how often people come close.

Part 3 · covers evaluating a policy when people are nearby

Success and stops are two axes, not one score

6 min live

Practitioner session 7 built the success-rate evaluation. Near people, that evaluation is half the picture. Run the stop evaluation separately, report stops by cause and by condition, and put the two side by side. When a new policy succeeds more and stops more near people, the data does not say it is better. It says there is a trade-off, and someone accountable has to decide it.

LiveReport stops by cause and condition3 min▶

A total stop rate hides the mechanism. Policy 0.5 in the constructed frontier might stop more because it moves faster through the shared zone, or because it reaches closer to the tote edge where people stand, or because its new grasp presses harder on parcels and trips the force limit with nobody present. Each of those is a different fix. Split stops by cause, then by condition (time of shift, which station, person present or not), exactly as practitioner session 7 splits success by condition.

Real world

Kress-Gazit et al. (2024) report re-running one policy on the same initial conditions after a lab reorganisation, and success dropped from 13/20 to 0/8. The policy had not changed; the room had. A shared cell changes every shift: who is on the floor, where they stand, how often they restock. A stop rate from a quiet week is a measurement of that week's people as much as of the policy.

LiveNear-misses are data2 min▶

A stop is recorded because a threshold was crossed. The situations that came close and did not cross it are invisible unless someone chooses to log them: a person inside the limited zone with no stop, a contact force just under the limit, an e-stop reached for and not pressed. Those are the leading indicator. Log the closest approach and the peak force per person entry, not only the trips, and the stop rate gains a distribution behind it.

Self-studyWhy "succeeds more" is not "better" by default2 min▶

The cost asymmetry from Part 1 is the reason. Six more successes per hundred trials and six more stops per hundred person entries are not exchangeable units, and no formula on this page converts one into the other. A data team's job is to make the trade-off visible, with intervals on both axes, and hand it to the people accountable for the cell. Practitioner session 7's caution applies twice here: both axes are estimated from small counts.

Build-along

A weekly stop report in pandas, from a constructed log

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Python 3.10 or later with numpy and pandas. You build Cell A's constructed week as a stop log of 40 rows with the record fields from Part 2, count stops by cause, compute the three rates and each cause's share, and print the one-line header every weekly stop report should start with. The figure shows where each printed number lands in the report.

Build the constructed stop log

Forty rows, one per protective stop, with the record fields from Part 2. Causes and the person-present flag are fixed so the counts match the widget; the times and robot states are drawn with seed 9. The exposure for the same week sits in a dictionary next to the log.

stops.py, part 1import numpy as np import pandas as pd rng = np.random.default_rng(9) # constructed Wrenfield week causes = (["zone entry"] * 26 + ["contact force limit"] * 6 + ["operator e-stop"] * 8) person = [True] * 26 + [True, True, False, False, False, False] + [True] * 5 + [False] * 3 log = pd.DataFrame({ "t": pd.Timestamp("2026-11-16 08:00") + pd.to_timedelta(np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 5 * 24 * 3600, 40)), unit="s"), "cause": causes, "robot_state": rng.choice(["moving", "grasping", "placing"], 40), "policy": "policy-0.5", "person_present": person, }) exposure = {"shared_hours": 50, "picks": 6000, "person_entries": 300}

Stops by cause and the three rates

Count by cause, then divide the same total by each exposure. Name the denominator in every printed line; a rate without one is the number from the start of the session that nobody could judge.

stops.py, part 2by_cause = log["cause"].value_counts() print(by_cause.to_string()) n = len(log) print("stops:", n) print("per shared hour:", round(n / exposure["shared_hours"], 2)) print("per 1,000 picks:", round(1000 * n / exposure["picks"], 2)) print("per 100 person entries:", round(100 * n / exposure["person_entries"], 2))

Per-cause share and the person-present count

The share says where to look first. The person-present count separates stops that involved someone from those that did not, such as a force-limit trip on a jammed parcel.

stops.py, part 3share = (100 * by_cause / n).round(1) print(share.to_string()) print("with a person present:", int(log["person_present"].sum()))

Write the report header

One line that travels with every chart and table from this week: the cell, the week, the policy, the count, all three exposures, and the word constructed.

stops.py, part 4print(f"WRENFIELD CELL A · week of {log['t'].min():%Y-%m-%d} · " f"{log['policy'].iloc[0]} · {n} stops · " f"{exposure['shared_hours']} shared h · {exposure['picks']} picks · " f"{exposure['person_entries']} entries · constructed")

Extend it: the denominator that answers your question

Add Cell B's exposure (2,500 picks, 30 shared hours, 600 entries) and 30 stops, and print both cells' rates side by side. Then add a limit_version column and confirm your report refuses to compare two weeks whose limit versions differ.

The habit to keep. No stop rate leaves the team without its denominator in the same sentence and its exposure in the header. "13.33 per 100 person entries, 300 entries, constructed week" can be argued with. "40 stops" cannot even be read.

Check yourself

Three questions

In the constructed Wrenfield week, Cell A has fewer stops per 1,000 picks than Cell B but more stops per 100 person entries. Why can both be true?

Same stops, different exposure. Each denominator answers a different question, which is why the question has to be chosen before the numbers are read.

Which set of fields makes a protective stop record usable as data?

Without time and policy version the stop cannot be joined to what the robot was doing or attributed to a policy; without the limit version, counts from different weeks are not comparable.

Why are ISO/TS 15066, ISO 10218 and Behrens et al. (2022) cited here as "reported", with no force or pressure values?

Quoting a number you have not read at source, from a table its own authors call preliminary, would turn a data lesson into an unverified compliance claim. The data consequence, record the limit version, holds whatever the values are.

Cheat sheet

The asymmetry. A missed grasp and a contact with a person are not one metric. Keep two ledgers.
The safety layer. Sits under the policy and writes the stops; a stop is exposure to risk, not an averted accident.
The record. Time, cause, robot state, policy version, limit version, person present.
Three denominators. Per shared hour, per pick, per person entry. Choose from the question.
The flip. Same stops, different exposure: two cells can swap places.
Intervals. Wilson for per-pick and per-entry proportions; overlapping intervals rank nothing.
Two axes. Success and stops near people side by side; more success with more stops is a trade-off.
Standards. Cited as reported, never quoted as limits. Not legal advice; record the limit version.

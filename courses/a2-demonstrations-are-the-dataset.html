Demonstrations are the dataset

In a warehouse the label is a column somebody already filled in. In imitation learning the label is a person driving a robot arm, one episode at a time, with someone resetting the table between takes. Tonight is what a demonstration actually records, what it costs in wall-clock hours rather than data minutes, which takes never reach training, and who quietly decides what the robot will ever see. Wrenfield Lab, the running case, is a constructed team; every Wrenfield number is generated in the page, not observed. Nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Wrenfield Lab, the week the demonstration budget is due

Wrenfield Lab is constructed: one two-arm robot at a packing bench, a small team, and an analyst who came from a retail warehouse. Last session she learned that the robot is a loop. This week finance asks a simpler question: "how much will the training data cost?" She opens the budget template and finds no line for it. There is no dataset to buy. The data will be made by people moving a pair of arms, at the bench, one parcel at a time, and the cost is mostly the minutes between the recordings.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A clear picture of teleoperation: a person moves a leader arm, a follower arm copies it, and the action written into the dataset is the leader's position, not the follower's. And a one-page collection brief you can hand to any team before they record a single episode.

LiveWhy the warehouse word "label" misleads here3 min▶

A warehouse analyst thinks of a label as a field: churned or not, fraud or not, filled in by a process that ran anyway. Labelling is a cleanup step after the data exists.

In imitation learning the data and the label are made in the same moment, by the same person. The robot learns to do what a person did, so every training example is a recording of a person doing the task through the robot. There is no cheaper source to fall back on, no backlog of historical episodes, and no way to label faster than a person can move an arm and reset a table. The demonstration is the expensive part, and it is also the part that sets the ceiling on what the policy can learn.

Real world

Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) built a two-arm teleoperation rig from low-cost off-the-shelf parts and collected 50 demonstrations per task. They report six real tasks learned to 80 to 90 percent success from about 10 minutes of demonstrations each. The same paper says those 10 to 20 minutes of data took 30 to 60 minutes of wall-clock time, because of resets and teleoperator mistakes. The headline is about data minutes; the budget is about rig minutes.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Imitation learning. Training a policy to copy what a person did in recorded demonstrations. The demonstrations are the labelled data.
  • Teleoperation. A person driving the robot from a distance or through a second device, so the robot's own sensors record the attempt.
  • Leader and follower. In a leader-follower rig a person moves the leader arm by hand and the follower arm, the one that touches the parcel, copies its joint positions.
  • Take. One recorded attempt during collection. A take becomes a training episode only if it passes the protocol and the success rule.
  • Wall-clock time. Time at the rig, including resets, retries and mistakes. Always larger than the seconds actually recorded.
  • Coverage. Which conditions the demonstrations span: lighting, parcel positions, parcel sizes, operators. A policy rarely does better than its coverage.
  • Constructed. Wrenfield Lab and its numbers are invented so the mechanism is visible. Research numbers are real and cited by author and year.
Part 1 · covers teleoperation and what the label is

The label is a person driving the arm

10 min live

Zhao and colleagues (2023) describe the cleanest version of the mechanism. A person holds a small leader arm and moves it. A follower arm, the one with the gripper and the parcel, copies the leader's joint positions, 50 times a second. Four cameras watch. When the episode is written down, the follower's joint readings and the four camera images go in as observations, and the leader's joint positions go in as the action. That choice is the label, and it is deliberate.

LiveWhy the action is read from the leader4 min▶

When the follower presses a parcel into a box, it cannot follow the leader exactly: the box pushes back. The leader, moved freely by a hand, goes a little further than the follower can. Zhao and colleagues record the leader's joint positions as the action because the force the robot applies is implicit in that difference, through the low-level controller. Record the follower's positions instead and you would teach the policy where the arm ended up, not what it had to ask for to get there.

For a leader signing off a dataset, the practical point is simple: "what is the action column, and which device was it read from?" is a question with a right answer, and a team should be able to give it in one sentence. Practitioner session 5 takes the action column apart in detail.

LiveTwo demonstrations of the same task are never the same3 min▶

Zhao and colleagues describe human demonstrations as "inherently stochastic", even when one person collects all of them: in a two-arm handover the position where the object changes hands is different in every episode. That is not an error to scrub out. It is the variation the policy has to learn to handle, and the paper's training method is built around it.

It also means a demonstration set has a style. One operator hands over high and early; another low and late. A policy trained on one person's demonstrations learns that person's habits, and nobody wrote that choice down. Part 3 returns to this as a hidden variable.

Self-studyReading "80 to 90 percent from 10 minutes" honestly4 min▶

The abstract of Zhao et al. (2023) reports six real-world tasks learned to 80 to 90 percent success with only 10 minutes worth of demonstrations. That is a real and influential result. Read it with three facts from the same paper beside it.

  • The trial count. Each real task was evaluated with 25 trials. A rate from 25 trials carries a wide uncertainty band: even 25 out of 25 has a 95 percent Wilson interval of about 87 to 100 percent (derived). Leader session 3 is about exactly this.
  • The wall-clock. "10 minutes of demonstrations" counts recorded data, not rig time. Per task that was 30 to 60 minutes at the bench.
  • The method mattered. The same paper shows that the training method, not only the data, made the difference: without one of its components, success on human data fell from 35.3 percent to 2 percent. Ten minutes of demonstrations is not a property of the data alone.
Part 2 · covers the cost of a demonstration, small and at scale

What a demonstration costs: count the rig minutes, not the data minutes

8 min live

A demonstration episode is short. In Zhao et al. (2023) each one took 8 to 14 seconds, 400 to 700 time steps at 50 Hz. The cost hides between episodes: someone resets the parcels, fixes a mistake, retries a take that went wrong. That is why 50 demonstrations per task were 10 to 20 minutes of data and 30 to 60 minutes at the rig. Both ends of that range are three times the data minutes (derived). The figure shows where the other two thirds went.

LiveReading the calculator at its defaults3 min▶

At the defaults, one task is 50 episodes of 12 seconds: 10 minutes of data and 30 minutes at the rig, the low end of what Zhao and colleagues report. Four tasks make 0.67 hours of data, 2.0 rig hours, 0.25 operator days and 20 cost units. Small, on purpose: the point is the ratio, not the total.

Now change one thing. Double the demonstrations per task and every line doubles. Change the overhead from 3 to 2 and the rig hours fall by a third, with no change to the data. The overhead is usually the lever a team can pull most cheaply: faster resets, a second set of parcels laid out in advance, fewer operator mistakes. Their user study adds one more: at 5 Hz instead of 50 Hz, teleoperation took 62 percent longer, so a laggy rig shows up directly in this calculator's overhead.

LiveThe same arithmetic at scale3 min▶

Khazatsky and colleagues (2024) collected DROID, a large in-the-wild manipulation dataset: 76k demonstration trajectories, about 350 hours of interaction, across 564 scenes, by 50 data collectors over 12 months, on the same hardware setup replicated 18 times. Two divisions put that in budget terms (both derived): 350 hours across 76k trajectories is about 17 seconds per trajectory, and 350 hours across 50 collectors is about 7 hours of recorded data each.

Seven recorded hours per person over a year sounds small until you apply Part 2's ratio. The recorded hours are the data, not the year of travel, setup, scene changes and resets behind them. The paper reports the data; the rest of the cost is the part a budget has to estimate.

Self-studyWhat the calculator leaves out3 min▶
  • Rejected takes. The calculator counts the demonstrations you keep. Part 3 shows the takes that are recorded and then excluded; they cost rig time too. Raise the overhead to cover them, or raise demonstrations per task.
  • Hardware time. A rig in use is a rig nobody else can evaluate on. Collection and evaluation compete for the same bench.
  • Learning curve. New operators make more mistakes. Zhao and colleagues name teleoperator mistakes as one reason for the overhead.
  • Coverage. The calculator assumes you know how many demonstrations a task needs. You find out partly by evaluating, which is leader session 3.
Part 3 · covers what gets thrown away and who collects

What never reaches training, and who decided

7 min live

Not every take becomes a training episode. In DROID, Khazatsky and colleagues (2024) had each collector mark every episode a success or a failure, excluded failed episodes from policy training, and released about 16k unsuccessful trajectories separately. Their protocol also aimed to prevent mistakes such as "camera cannot see robot" and "teleoperator in camera view". The funnel is where a dataset's shape is decided, and much of it is decided by the person holding the leader arm.

LiveWhy keep the failures you will not train on3 min▶

Failed takes are excluded from training because imitation learning copies what is in the data: teach it a failure and it learns to fail. But the failures are the best map of where the task is hard. If most failed takes cluster at the table edge or in dim light, that is the condition the policy will struggle with too, and the condition that needs more demonstrations. DROID kept that evidence and released it separately rather than deleting it.

The warehouse parallel is a rejected-records table. Nobody trains a revenue model on malformed orders, and nobody sensible deletes them either; they tell you where the pipeline breaks. Each failed take should carry a reason, not just a cross.

LiveThe collector is a hidden variable2 min▶

Two things in the funnel are not gates at all. Operator style: demonstrations are "inherently stochastic" even from one person (Zhao et al. 2023), and different operators move differently, so a set collected mostly by one person teaches that person's habits. Coverage: which lighting, which parcel positions near the edges, which parcel sizes. Whoever collects decides, usually by what is convenient that afternoon. The protocol check catches a camera that cannot see the robot; nothing catches a dataset that never saw a dim evening.

The fix is managerial, not statistical: write the coverage down before collection, rotate operators across it, and record which operator and which condition produced each take. That is the build-along.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Whether collecting demonstrations at ever larger scale is, on its own, the route to general-purpose robots is an open question, and people close to the work disagree. When such robots arrive is contested too; no page here gives a date.

The published evidence is narrower and mixed. The Open X-Embodiment collaboration (2023) pooled more than 1M real robot trajectories from 60 datasets across 22 robot embodiments. A model trained on the pool did 50 percent better on average than the original methods on small-data robots, and on large-data robots it underfit and did worse. More demonstrations helped some robots and not others in the same study (practitioner session 4). A leader should hear "it depends on the robot, the data and the model" as the honest answer for now, and fund coverage and quality alongside volume.

Build-along

Wrenfield's one-page demonstration collection brief

★ 17 min · everyone builds

No code. You write the brief Wrenfield's team would hand every operator before the first take: what tasks, what counts as success, which conditions must be covered, how operators rotate, what to log when a take is rejected, and what it will cost. The figure is a filled example; the grid on the right is the part most briefs leave out.

List the tasks, one line each

Name each task as an operator would hear it: "pick a parcel from the tray", "pack it in the box", "hand it to the other arm". If a task needs two sentences, it is two tasks. Wrenfield's example has three to four; yours may have more.

Write the success rule before anyone records

One written rule per task, specific enough that two people would mark the same take the same way: "parcel inside the box, lid line clear, nothing dropped". The collector marks each take against it, as DROID's collectors did. Leader session 3 shows why the person who wrote the rule should not be the only one judging it.

Draw the coverage grid

Rows are the conditions the robot will meet: lighting (dim, normal), positions (centre, near the edges), parcel sizes (small, large). Columns are operators. Mark how many demonstrations each cell gets. A blank cell is a decision; make it on purpose, not by convenience.

Set the operator rotation

Every condition row should have more than one operator, so the policy learns the task and not one person's style. Record the operator id on every take. Then a later failure can be traced to a condition, an operator or both.

Decide what to log for rejects

Every rejected take gets a reason code, not just a cross: "camera cannot see robot", "teleoperator in camera view", "parcel dropped", "reset incomplete", "task failed". Keep the failed takes, as DROID did, in a separate store. They are where the task is hard.

Put the budget in from the calculator

Enter your tasks, demonstrations per task and episode length into the calculator in Part 2. Copy the data hours, rig hours and operator days onto the brief, and write the overhead you assumed next to them. If your rejects will be frequent, raise the overhead rather than pretending they are free. Your numbers will differ from Wrenfield's.

The habit to keep. Before approving any demonstration budget, ask for three things on one page: the success rule, the coverage grid with operators across it, and the rig hours rather than the data hours. A team that cannot produce the grid will collect whatever was convenient, and the robot will learn exactly that.

Check yourself

Three questions

In a leader-follower teleoperation rig like the one in Zhao et al. (2023), which reading is recorded as the action?

The follower's positions and the camera images are observations. The leader's positions are what the person commanded; recording the follower instead would teach where the arm ended up, not what it had to ask for.

Zhao and colleagues report 10 to 20 minutes of demonstration data per task but 30 to 60 minutes of wall-clock time. Why the gap?

The paper names resets and teleoperator mistakes. Both ends of the range are about 3x the data minutes, which is why a budget should count rig minutes.

DROID excluded failed episodes from policy training. Why release about 16k unsuccessful trajectories separately instead of deleting them?

Training on failures teaches the policy to fail, so they stay out of the training set. Kept separately, they are a map of hard conditions, the robot data equivalent of a rejected-records table.

Before session 3

Homework

Twenty minutes, on a budget and a grid.

Price one task you know

Pick any physical task you understand (packing an order, sorting returns). Estimate seconds per attempt and a reset time, then put them in the calculator. Write the overhead you think is honest and the one sentence that justifies it.

Find the hole in your grid

Draw a coverage grid for that task: four conditions by three people. Mark the cell your team would most likely skip because it is inconvenient, and write what the robot would do the first time it meets that condition. Leader session 3 asks how many trials it would take to notice.

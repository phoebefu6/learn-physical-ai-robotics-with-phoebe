From warehouse to physical AI: the transfer map

A conventional data analyst, engineer or scientist does not start from zero near a robot. Some of what they already do is worth more here than it was in the warehouse, some of it is genuinely new, and a few habits are actively dangerous when the model's output moves an arm. Tonight sorts all of it into three columns: carries over, must learn, must unlearn. Then it turns the map into a 90-day self-study ladder on free and official sources. Wrenfield Lab, the parcel-packing team in every session, is constructed; every Wrenfield number is generated by a seeded engine, not observed. The research numbers are real and cited.

Part 0

Wrenfield Lab, the analyst's first performance review

Wrenfield Lab is constructed. The analyst who arrived from a retail warehouse five sessions ago is asked a fair question by her manager: which of your old skills are you still using, what did you have to learn, and what did you have to stop doing? Her honest answer surprises both of them. Most of her toolkit survived. Her quality checks, her habit of slicing every metric, her confidence intervals and her obsession with lineage are the reason the team trusts its numbers. What nearly hurt them were five reflexes she never thought of as reflexes. Tonight is her answer, drawn as a map anyone arriving from a data team can use.

LiveWhy a transfer map, not a reading list3 min▶

People moving into robotics from a data team are usually handed a reading list, and the reading list assumes they know nothing. That wastes their strongest skills and hides their real risks. A data engineer already knows how to quarantine a bad batch; the new thing is that the batch is an episode with three cameras on one clock. A data scientist already knows a rate needs a denominator; the new thing is that the denominator is twenty trials, each reset by hand.

The dangerous part is the third column. Habits that were harmless in a warehouse, such as treating configuration as something outside the data, or deleting the failed rows before analysis, do real damage when the data comes from a machine whose own actions shaped what was recorded.

Real world

The Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) pooled more than a million real robot trajectories from 22 robot embodiments and 60 datasets. The authors are frank about what that took and what it left undone: one canonical camera view per dataset, actions normalized per dataset, and in their words "we do not align the coordinate frames across datasets". That sentence is a lineage problem at the scale of a field, and it is exactly the kind of problem a data team already knows how to name.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Carries over. A skill you already have that is worth more near a robot, usually because each data point is expensive.
  • Must learn. Knowledge and tools a warehouse never needed: episodes, coordinate frames, demonstrations, simulation, cross-robot data.
  • Must unlearn. A habit that was harmless in a warehouse and is actively dangerous here.
  • Episode. One attempt at a task, every camera and joint on one clock, plus a success flag a person decided (practitioner session 1).
  • Calibration. The measured position and properties of each camera relative to the robot. Part of every row, whether or not it is recorded (practitioner session 2).
  • Primary source. The paper or dataset documentation written by the people who did the work. Free on arXiv for every paper on this page.
  • Official source. The body that owns a thing: a dataset's own documentation, or the standards organisation's catalogue page for a standard.
  • Constructed. Wrenfield Lab and every number about it are invented so that the truth is knowable. The research numbers are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers what carries over from a data team

Four skills you already have, worth more here

8 min live

The first column is good news. Four things a conventional data team does well are not just useful near a robot; they are scarcer and more valuable, because every real data point costs a person's time. The figure draws the three columns and fills only the first. The other two stay dashed until Parts 2 and 3.

LiveData contracts and slicing, pointed at robots4 min▶

Data contracts and quality checks. A warehouse contract says which columns arrive, in which types, with which keys. The same discipline applied to an episode becomes five checks before anything reaches training: the clock has no holes, every camera is present and saw the robot, the action column is the command that was sent, the identity bundle is complete, and the outcome has a written rule. Practitioner session 1 builds them. The skill is the same; the unit is a time-indexed bundle instead of a row.

Slicing by condition. A warehouse analyst never trusts an overall conversion rate before slicing it by region and channel. Near a robot the slices are lighting, position on the bench and starting condition. In Wrenfield's constructed bench, the camera-randomized policy scores 46 of 50 overall, which looks finished. Sliced, it is 29 of 29 in the centre of the bench and 17 of 21 at the edge. That edge slice is how the team found a lens the simulator did not model. Twenty real demonstrations took the edge to 20 of 21 (practitioner session 10).

LiveIntervals and lineage, with smaller numbers and wider identity4 min▶

Confidence intervals and honest comparisons. In the warehouse the interval was so narrow nobody printed it. Here it is the headline. Wrenfield's constructed claim from leader session 3, 18 of 20 against 15 of 20, gives Wilson intervals of 69.9 to 97.2 percent and 53.1 to 88.8 percent, and Fisher's exact test gives p = 0.4075. The forty trials cannot rank the two policies. A data scientist who already reaches for the interval is the most useful person in a go or no-go meeting.

Lineage. A warehouse engineer asks which version of which pipeline produced a row. Near a robot the question widens: which robot, which calibration of which camera, which policy, which operator, which success rule. Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) report a policy re-run from the same initial conditions after a lab reorganization falling from 13 of 20 to 0 of 8. The policy file had not changed. Without lineage that includes the room, nobody could have said why.

Self-studyWhy these four are worth more, not the same3 min▶

Each real data point near a robot costs a person's time. Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) collected 10 to 20 minutes of demonstration data per task, and it took 30 to 60 minutes of wall-clock time because of resets and operator mistakes. When a team can afford tens of trials, not millions of rows, a check that saves one bad episode, a slice that spots one failure mode, or an interval that stops one false ranking is worth far more than it was in a warehouse where data was free and refreshed weekly.

The skill transfers. The confidence does not have to: the work is the same shape with smaller samples, and a data person who says "with twenty trials I cannot tell" is giving the most valuable sentence in the room.

LiveRungs 1 to 3: episodes, frames, demonstrations4 min▶

Episodes and which column is the action. In the two-arm teleoperation rig of Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023), the observation is the follower arms' joint positions plus four cameras, and the action recorded is the leader arms' joint positions, the ones the person moved. The force the robot applies is implicit in the difference between the two. A data person who flattens that into one table and picks the wrong column as the label trains a model to predict the wrong thing, and nothing errors.

Coordinate frames and calibration. A camera sees pixels; the arm moves in its own coordinates. A calibration connects them, and it is part of every row. The arithmetic is short: 1 degree of camera yaw at a 60 cm working distance moves the apparent point by 600 x tan(1 degree) = 10.47 mm, about 1.05 cm. Wrenfield's constructed grasp tolerance is 1.0 cm, so a 1 degree error is already a miss.

Demonstrations and their cost. The label is a person driving the arm. Zhao and colleagues used 50 demonstrations per task; 10 to 20 minutes of data took 30 to 60 minutes of wall-clock time. Who collected, what they threw away and how the takes differ are data questions (leader session 2, practitioner session 3).

LiveRungs 4 and 5: simulation and data from other robots4 min▶

Simulation and domain randomization, checked on the real robot. Tobin and colleagues (2017) trained a detector on simulated images with random textures, lights, distractors and camera positions; on real images it located objects to within 1.5 cm, and a robot using it picked the target in 38 of 40 trials. The check that mattered was on the real robot. Wrenfield's constructed bench shows the same shape: no randomization gives 29 of 50 real grasps while the simulator says 100 percent; randomizing lighting and camera pose takes the real table to 46 of 50.

Cross-robot data. The Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) reports that a model trained on the pooled data had a mean success rate 50 percent higher than the original methods on robots with small datasets, and underfit and did worse on robots with large ones. Pooling helps some robots and hurts others in the same study. Practitioner session 4 teaches the disagreement rather than a rule.

Self-studyWhy the ladder has this order3 min▶

Each rung is a question the next rung cannot answer for you. Frames are meaningless until you know which column they transform. A demonstration's quality depends on whether its camera calibration and action column are right. A simulator can only be checked against real episodes you trust. And pooled data from many robots carries every one of those problems at once, which is why the Open X-Embodiment authors list camera observations that "still vary substantially" and actions that may be absolute or relative, positions or velocities.

A data person who climbs out of order usually starts at rung 5, because big pooled datasets look like the warehouse. It is the hardest rung to judge without the four below it.

Part 3 · covers the habits that must be unlearned

Five warehouse habits that are dangerous near an arm

7 min live

The third column is the one nobody writes down, because these habits feel like common sense. Each was harmless, even efficient, in a warehouse. Each breaks near a robot for a reason you can draw. The figure puts the warehouse habit on the left, in red, and what replaces it on the right.

LiveThree habits about what the data means4 min▶

"The data records the world." A warehouse row records an event that would have happened whether or not anyone measured it. A robot's data records what its policy did and what followed. Change the policy and the next log changes with it, so a dataset is always a dataset of some policy (leader session 5, practitioner session 8).

"More rows fix it." Some gaps only real data can close. Wrenfield's constructed real lens magnifies by 8 percent and no simulator setting knew it; widening the simulator could not teach it, and twenty real demonstrations took the edge slice from 17 of 21 to 20 of 21. At the other end, the Open X-Embodiment authors report that pooling more data made their model underfit on robots that already had large datasets. More rows is a hypothesis, not a fix.

"98 percent is a result." Not without the trial count. Wrenfield's best constructed rung is 49 of 50, 98 percent, with a Wilson interval of 89.5 to 99.6 percent. The same 98 percent from thousands of trials would be a different claim. On leader session 3's example, 18 of 20 against 15 of 20 is p = 0.41: a 15-point headline gap that forty trials cannot confirm.

LiveTwo habits about what the data includes3 min▶

"Config is not data." In a warehouse, a config file lives in a repository and the data lives in a table. Near a robot the camera's position is a property of every row. In Wrenfield's constructed bench, moving the real camera 2 cm without recording it took the best policy from 49 of 50 to 0 of 50. Nothing in the episode table changed shape; every number in it was quietly wrong (practitioner session 2 has the break button).

"Drop the failures." Cleaning failed rows before analysis feels tidy. Khazatsky and colleagues (2024) had collectors mark every episode as a success or a failure, excluded the failures from policy training, and still released about 16 thousand unsuccessful trajectories separately. The failures are where the next policy's weaknesses are written down. Keep them, label them, and decide per use whether they belong.

Self-studyWhere the stakes come from3 min▶

In a warehouse, an unnoticed data error costs a wrong report. Near a moving arm, and especially near people, the cost can be physical. The collaborative-robot standards, ISO/TS 15066 and the 2025 revision of ISO 10218 (both reported; the texts are paywalled), describe force-limited operation and protective stops. This is not legal advice and not a compliance checklist. The data consequence is the one to keep: every protective stop and force-limit trip is a logged event with a cost, and a team that drops those rows or does not record the calibration they happened under cannot learn from them (practitioner session 9).

Build-along

A 90-day self-study ladder on free and official sources

★ 17 min · everyone builds

No code tonight; the ladder ends with some. Turn the must-learn column into twelve weeks of evenings, using only free sources, and know which of them are primary or official. The figure is the plan. The steps write it into your own calendar, and the last step turns tonight's three columns into your own map.

Block three four-week windows in your calendar

Weeks 1 to 4, 5 to 8, 9 to 12. Pick the same evenings each week; the order matters more than the hours. Write the three block titles from the figure into the calendar entries so each evening starts with a question, not a search.

Weeks 1 to 4: read the five primary papers, one question each

All free on arXiv, all primary: Tobin et al. (2017), arXiv 1703.06907, ask what was randomized and how it was checked on a real robot. Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023), arXiv 2304.13705, ask which column is the action. Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023), arXiv 2310.08864, ask what was not aligned. Khazatsky et al. (2024), arXiv 2403.12945, ask what happened to the failures. Kress-Gazit et al. (2024), arXiv 2409.09491, ask who should judge success.

Weeks 5 to 8: open an open robot dataset and load one episode

Choose any openly released robot dataset and read its own documentation first; that documentation is the official source for its format, its action column and its cameras, and it is free. Load a single episode with a dataframe library and run practitioner session 1's five checks on it: clock, cameras, action, identity, outcome. Write down every field the documentation names that the episode does not contain.

Weeks 9 to 12: randomize in a physics simulator and evaluate in 20 trials

Run any free physics simulator on a simple pick task. Train or script a policy with randomized lighting and camera pose, then hold one setting out of training and evaluate on it in 20 trials. Report the result as k of 20 with a Wilson interval, the way practitioner session 7 does, and compare it with the same policy on the training settings. Your numbers will differ from anyone else's; the habit is the point.

Alongside, any week: find the standards at their official source

The standards body's own catalogue page is the official source for ISO 10218-1 and -2 (2025) and ISO/TS 15066 (2016). Read the scope and status there. The standards themselves are paywalled, so say "reported" for anything you have not read in the text. Not legal advice: the aim is to know which stops and force limits your logs should record.

Draw your own three columns and pick your sessions

On one page, copy the three column headings and fill them with your own skills and habits, not tonight's. Then tick the self-assessment in Part 2 honestly and write its session list under your map. That list, in that order, is what runs alongside your 90 days.

The habit to keep. When you learn from a source, write down whether it is primary, official or secondary, and whether you read it or read about it. It is the same discipline as a k of n next to a rate: the claim travels with where it came from.

Check yourself

Three questions

Carries over. Wrenfield's camera-randomized policy (constructed) scores 46 of 50 overall. Which warehouse skill found the problem hiding inside that number?

Slicing is the analyst's oldest habit and it transfers directly. The edge slice located the problem; twenty real demonstrations then took the edge to 20 of 21. More simulation could not have, because the simulator did not know about the lens.

Must learn. In the teleoperation rig of Zhao et al. (2023), which column is recorded as the action?

The follower's joint positions and the cameras are the observation. The action is the leader's joint positions; the force the robot applies is implicit in the difference between leader and follower. Picking the wrong column as the label is a silent error.

Must unlearn. Wrenfield's best policy (constructed) falls from 49 of 50 to 0 of 50 overnight. Nothing in the episode table changed shape. Which warehouse habit hid the cause?

The camera's position is part of every row. When it moves without a record, every number in the table is quietly wrong and nothing errors. Recording calibration as versioned data is what lets a team see that the room changed, not the policy.

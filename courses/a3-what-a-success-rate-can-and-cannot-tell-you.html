What a success rate can and cannot tell you

Robot policies are judged on tens of real trials, because every trial needs a person to reset the table. That makes the success rate the most quoted and most over-read number in the field. Tonight is what the number needs next to it before anyone acts on it: the trial count and its interval, the written rule for success and who applied it, and the conditions the trials ran under. Wrenfield Lab, the parcel-packing team in every session, is constructed; every Wrenfield number is generated by a seeded engine in the page, not observed. The research numbers are real and cited.

Part 0

Wrenfield Lab, the morning of the go or no-go meeting

Wrenfield Lab is constructed. The grasp team has a new policy and a slide that says "new policy 90 percent, old policy 75 percent, ship it". The analyst from the warehouse is asked to agree. In her old job a 15-point lift on a dashboard of a million orders was a finding. Here each percentage is twenty real attempts, run on one afternoon, scored by the engineer who wrote the new policy, under a success rule nobody wrote down. The number is the same shape as the one she knows. The evidence behind it is not.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Why a rate without its trial count is not a result: 18 of 20 has a 95 percent interval of 69.9 to 97.2 percent, 15 of 20 has 53.1 to 88.8 percent, and the two overlap so much that Fisher's exact test gives p = 0.4075. Why "success" is a rule somebody wrote, and why the person who wrote the policy should not be the one who applies it. Why the conditions move the rate more than the policy sometimes does: one published re-run fell from 13 of 20 to 0 of 8 after a lab was rearranged. And a five-slot sentence that every robot rate you sign off should fit into: k of n, interval, conditions, rule, who judged.

LiveWhy the warehouse instinct misfires here3 min▶

A warehouse analyst reads a rate as nearly exact because the denominator is huge and free. A conversion rate over a million sessions has an interval so narrow nobody prints it. Robot evaluation is the opposite: each trial costs a person's time to reset the scene, so the denominator is small and the interval is wide.

Two other habits also misfire. In the warehouse, "converted" is defined by the system, not by a person watching. In robotics a person usually decides whether a grasp counted. And in the warehouse, last month and this month are comparable unless something big changed. In a robot cell, moving a lamp or a shelf can change the result more than changing the policy.

Real world

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) surveyed how robot learning papers report results and found that they usually give a success rate with little on the number of runs, the initial conditions, the success criteria or any statistical analysis. Their recommendation is to report all four, plus complementary metrics and the ways the policy failed. Tonight is that recommendation, read as a decision-maker.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Trial. One attempt by the policy from a set starting position, ending in a success or a failure. Also called a rollout when the policy runs on its own.
  • k of n. Successes out of trials. Always written as the pair, never only as the percentage.
  • 95 percent interval. The range of true success rates that are consistent with the k of n you saw. Narrow with many trials, wide with few.
  • Wilson interval. A way to compute that range that stays sensible at 0 of n and n of n, where the textbook formula collapses to zero width (Wilson 1927, reported).
  • Fisher's exact test. A standard test of whether two small success counts differ by more than chance would explain. It gives a p-value; a large one means "these trials cannot rank the two".
  • Initial conditions. Where the parcel starts, how the light falls, what else is on the table. Two policies compared from different starts are not compared at all.
  • Distribution shift. The world the policy meets has moved away from the world it was trained and tested in. The rate moves with it.
  • Constructed. Wrenfield Lab and every number about it are invented so that the truth is knowable. The research numbers are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers trial counts, Wilson intervals and comparing two policies

A rate needs its trial count, and the count sets the width

9 min live

90 percent from 10 trials and 90 percent from 100 trials are the same number and very different evidence. The way to see the difference is the interval: the range of true rates that could plausibly have produced what you saw. With twenty trials each, Wrenfield's two policies have intervals that overlap across most of their length. The marigold band is the region where both could live.

Live9 of 10 is not 90 of 1004 min▶

9 of 10 has a Wilson 95 percent interval of 59.6 to 98.2 percent. Everything from "fails four times in ten" to "almost never fails" fits those ten trials. Type 90 and 100 into Policy A in the widget and watch the interval close in around 90: same rate, about ten times the work, a much narrower claim.

Research practice lives at the small end. Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) evaluated each real task of their two-arm teleoperation system with 25 trials. Khazatsky and colleagues (2024), the team behind the DROID dataset, ran 10 rollouts per task setting per method in their policy comparisons. Neither is careless; real trials are that expensive. Even a perfect 25 of 25 only says the true rate is above about 86.7 percent.

Tobin and colleagues (2017) reported 38 grasps in 40 cluttered trials for a detector trained only in simulation. Derived from their counts, the interval is 83.5 to 98.6 percent: strong, and still a range, not a point.

LiveReading two policies side by side3 min▶

When two intervals overlap heavily, do not rank the policies on their point rates. Fisher's exact test asks the question directly: if the two policies were equally good, how often would forty trials split at least this unevenly? At 18 of 20 against 15 of 20 the answer is p = 0.4075, about two times in five. That is not evidence of a difference.

The usual convention reads p below 0.05 as "unlikely to be chance alone". Treat it as a convention, not a law, and remember that a small p says nothing about whether the trials were run fairly. Part 3 is about that.

Self-studyWhy Wilson and not the textbook interval4 min▶

The interval most people learned, rate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, is called the Wald interval. At 0 of n and n of n its standard error is zero, so it reports an interval of zero width: "exactly 0 percent" or "exactly 100 percent" from twenty trials. That is the one place a robotics team most needs honesty, because perfect and zero scores are common at small n.

Wilson's interval (1927, reported; the formula is standard) does not collapse. 0 of 20 gives 0 to 16.1 percent; 20 of 20 gives 83.9 to 100 percent. The engine on this page and the bench in practitioner session 10 use it, and the engine checks itself against a planted Wald fault every time the page loads.

Part 2 · covers the definition of success and who applies it

Whose success? The rule is part of the number

6 min live

A success flag looks like a fact recorded by the robot. It is a judgement made by a person applying a rule. Change the rule and the same recorded episode moves from one column to the other. The figure takes one constructed Wrenfield episode and judges it twice.

LiveWrite the rule before the trials, and hand it to someone else3 min▶

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) ask for "a clear, detailed, and unambiguous definition of success", written before evaluation. They also split the roles: the person who designed the behaviour writes the criteria, but does not act as the evaluator. The designer knows what the policy was meant to do and will, without meaning to, read borderline cases in its favour.

For a leader the test is simple. Ask to see the rule as written text, with a version, and ask who applied it. "The engineer watched and ticked" is not an evaluation; it is a demonstration.

Live13 of 20 against 14 of 20: the honest sentence3 min▶

The same paper works an example with two policies on a food-handling task: policy A succeeds 13 times in 20, policy B 14 times in 20. Their conclusion is plain: "we cannot state that one policy outperforms the other".

The numbers agree. Derived with the engine, 13 of 20 has an interval of 43.3 to 81.9 percent and 14 of 20 has 48.1 to 85.5 percent, and Fisher's exact test gives p = 1.0. Type 13, 20, 14, 20 into the widget above. A one-trial difference at twenty trials is no difference at all, and the right decision is either more trials or a different question.

Self-studyVersion the rule like you version the policy4 min▶

Rules change for good reasons. Wrenfield's constructed v1 counted a lift as success because lifting was the hard part last quarter; v2 counts an upright parcel in the tote because packing is the job now. Both are reasonable. Mixing them is not.

  • Store the rule version on every flag, not in a slide. A dashboard that charts success over time across a rule change shows a drop that is really a redefinition.
  • Re-judge, do not re-label. If episodes are re-scored under a new rule, keep both flags and say which one a chart uses.
  • Keep the failure reasons. Kress-Gazit and colleagues also recommend reporting how a policy failed, not only how often. "Dropped in carry" and "landed on its side" call for different fixes.
Part 3 · covers initial conditions, distribution shift and evaluation practice

Conditions move the rate, sometimes more than the policy does

10 min live

A success rate belongs to a policy and a world. Change where the parcels start, how the light falls, or what stands next to the bench, and the same policy earns a different rate. That is why fair comparisons run the policies on matched starting conditions in one session. It is also why even a fair twenty-trial comparison can fail to separate policies that really differ: Wrenfield's bench, where the truth is known, shows it.

Live13 of 20 became 0 of 8 after the lab moved4 min▶

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) report re-running policy A from their example on the same initial conditions after the lab had been reorganized. Success fell from 13 of 20 to 0 of 8. The policy had not changed. The room had, and the policy met a world it had not been trained or tested in: distribution shift.

Two lessons for a leader. First, an old evaluation does not certify a policy in a changed cell; the rate travels with the conditions, not with the model file. Second, comparing a new policy run this week against an old policy's number from last month is not a comparison. Something in the room may have moved, and you will not be able to tell which change produced the gap.

LiveThree practices that make a comparison fair3 min▶
  • Match the initial conditions. Start each policy from the same parcel positions and scene, using image overlays or floor markings so the setups really are the same (Kress-Gazit et al. 2024).
  • Interleave the policies, blind to the evaluator. Run A, B, B, A in a shuffled order and do not tell the person judging which policy is running.
  • Run every policy in one session. Same day, same light, same table, so that a change in the room hits all policies alike instead of landing on one of them.

None of these needs more trials. They make the trials you can afford mean what the slide says they mean.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

How to analyse a small set of robot trials is not settled. One tradition uses frequentist tools: confidence intervals like Wilson's and tests like Fisher's, the ones on this page. Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) work their examples with Bayesian analysis instead, starting from a uniform prior over the success rate and reporting what the trials do to it, and mention frequentist intervals as the alternative.

Both are legitimate and both are used by careful people. They answer slightly different questions and they agree on the big picture at the sizes on this page: twenty trials leave a lot of uncertainty. The mistake that matters is not choosing the wrong school; it is reporting a bare percentage with no analysis at all.

Self-studyThe bench says the same thing3 min▶

Wrenfield's bench (constructed) can afford 2,000 trials per rung because it is a simulation of a real cell, so it knows each rung's true rate. At 20 trials with seed 1122, rungs 2, 3 and 4 score 18, 19 and 20 of 20 while their true rates are 0.716, 0.908 and 0.951. The worst rung would win a twenty-trial demo day with 90 percent.

A real team never gets the 2,000-trial column. That is the point of the interval: it is the honest stand-in for the truth you cannot see. Practitioner session 7 takes the same row apart with paired conditions, and practitioner session 10 lets you change the trial count yourself.

Build-along

Rewrite three claims into five slots

★ 17 min · everyone builds

No code. Below are three constructed Wrenfield claims of the kind that reach a go or no-go meeting. Rewrite each one into a sentence with five slots: k of n, interval, conditions, rule, who judged. Use the widget in Part 1 for the interval and the comparison. Where a slot cannot be filled, write "unknown"; an unknown slot is itself the finding.

Draw the five slots on a page

Five rows: k of n, interval, conditions, rule, who judged. Every claim below gets one copy of the grid. Leave the rows empty for now; the exercise is noticing which ones the claim fills by itself.

Claim 1: "The new grasp policy is 90 percent, the old one 75 percent"

Constructed: Wrenfield ran each policy 20 times. Put 18 of 20 and 15 of 20 into the widget, copy both intervals and the p-value into the grid, and write the one-line verdict the widget gives. Compare your page with the figure above.

Claim 2: "The packing policy never failed in testing"

Constructed: it was 20 of 20. Type 20 and 20 into Policy A. Write the interval down and turn "never fails" into "in 20 trials it did not fail; the true rate is probably above about 84 percent". Then ask what it was never tested on.

Claim 3: "The old drawer policy is useless, zero percent"

Constructed: it was 0 of 20. The widget gives an interval of 0 to 16.1 percent. That is informative: the policy very probably works less than one time in six. It is not proof of exactly zero, and it says nothing about a different room.

Fill conditions, rule and judge, or write "unknown"

For each claim, ask the three questions the numbers cannot answer. Were the policies run from matched starts in one session, interleaved? Which written rule, which version? Was the judge the person who built the policy? Every "unknown" goes on the page in the same ink as the numbers.

Write the sentence you would sign

One line per claim, all five slots in it. Then pick the decision it supports: ship, do not ship, or run more trials under a fairer setup. For claim 1 the honest decision is the third, and the cost of it is another afternoon of resets, not another quarter.

The habit to keep. Never repeat a robot success rate as a percentage alone. Say it as k of n with its interval, under named conditions, by a written rule, judged by someone who did not build the policy. If you cannot fill a slot, that gap is what you report.

Check yourself

Three questions

Wrenfield's new policy (constructed) scores 18 of 20, the old one 15 of 20. What can the team say?

Forty trials split this unevenly about two times in five even if the policies were equally good. The honest options are more trials under matched conditions, or a decision made on other grounds.

A policy scores 0 of 20. What does that tell you?

The Wilson interval for 0 of 20 runs from 0 to 16.1 percent. The textbook Wald interval would collapse to zero width there, which is why Wilson is used. And the result belongs to the conditions it was measured in.

Kress-Gazit et al. (2024) re-ran a policy from the same initial conditions after the lab was reorganized, and success fell from 13 of 20 to 0 of 8. What is the best reading?

The policy had not changed; the world around it had. Zero in eight after thirteen in twenty is far too large a fall to put down to chance, and the authors attribute it to the shift. A rate travels with its conditions.

Homework

Put one published robot rate into the five slots

Pick a success rate from any robotics paper or public statement. Fill k of n, compute the interval with the widget, and write what the source says about conditions, the success rule and who judged. Mark every slot the source leaves empty.

Find a rate in your own work that lost its denominator

Any small-sample percentage you or your team have reported: a pilot, a survey, an A/B test with a few hundred users. Cheat sheet

The one sentence. A robot success rate is k of n, an interval, the conditions, the rule and the judge.
Count first. 9 of 10 runs from 59.6 to 98.2 percent. The percentage alone hides that.

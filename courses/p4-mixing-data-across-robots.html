Mixing data across robots

Every robot team eventually asks whether someone else's data can help. Tonight is about what has to be true before it can. A robot's body, its gripper, where its cameras sit and how it reads a command all change what one row of data means. You will see the coarse alignment a large research collaboration used to pool 60 datasets, the one step that keeps a borrowed dataset from drowning yours, and the honest result: pooling helped some robots and hurt others. Wrenfield Lab and all its numbers are constructed; the research numbers are real and cited.

Part 0

Wrenfield is offered a borrowed dataset

Wrenfield Lab (constructed) has a few hundred parcel-pick episodes from its own two-arm robot. A partner team offers ten times more episodes from a different arm doing similar picks. The tempting move is to concatenate the tables and retrain. Tonight is about why that can quietly turn your robot into a worse copy of theirs, and the checks that decide whether pooling is a gift or a dilution.

LiveWhy "more data" is not automatically better here3 min▶

In a web or warehouse dataset, more rows of the same schema usually means a better estimate. Robot data from another robot has the same column names and a different meaning. The action column says "move 0.05 along x", but whose x, in what unit, measured from where, and is it a position or a speed? Pool without answering and the model learns an average of incompatible instructions.

Real world

The Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) pooled more than 1 million real robot trajectories: 60 datasets from 21 institutions, covering 22 robot embodiments and 527 skills (160,266 tasks). It is the largest published attempt to train one robot policy on many bodies, and the authors are unusually candid about what they could not reconcile. Most of tonight is built on what they wrote.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Embodiment. The robot's body as the data sees it: arm geometry, gripper, camera placement and control scheme.
  • End-effector. The business end of the arm, usually the gripper. An end-effector action says where the gripper should go, not what each joint should do.
  • Normalisation statistics. The per-dataset mean and standard deviation of each action dimension, stored next to the data so a prediction can be turned back into real units.
  • Pooling. Training one model on several datasets at once.
  • Underfitting. The model is too small or too constrained to fit what the data contains, so it does worse even on data it has seen plenty of.
Part 1 · covers embodiment and why one action means many motions

The same action vector, three different motions

7 min live

Embodiment is four things the data inherits from the body that made it: the arm's geometry (link lengths, reach, joint limits), the gripper (two fingers, suction, width), where the cameras sit (on the wrist, overhead, to the side) and the control scheme (what a number in the action column instructs the controller to do). Change any one and the same numbers mean a different motion.

LiveThe authors' own words3 min▶

The clearest statement of the problem comes from the people who pooled the most data. Writing about their own coarse alignment, the Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) warn that "the same action vector may induce very different motions for different robots". They pooled anyway, with eyes open, and measured what happened. That is the professional stance: a known mismatch is a risk you measure, not a reason to pretend the columns agree.

Real world

The pooled collection spans 22 robot embodiments: different arm geometries, grippers, camera placements and control schemes, gathered across 21 institutions. Each of the 60 datasets was recorded by a team optimising for its own robot, not for anyone pooling it later.

LiveThe four parts of embodiment, as data columns3 min▶
  • Arm geometry. Decides which positions are reachable. A target one arm reaches easily may be outside another's workspace, so the same demonstration cannot be replayed.
  • Gripper. Decides what "close" means: a width, a force, a binary flag. Store it with its unit.
  • Camera placement. Decides what the policy sees. A wrist camera moves with the arm; a fixed one does not. Practitioner session 2 covers what one camera move does to every episode after it.
  • Control scheme. Decides what a number in the action column instructs: a position, a change in position, or a speed. Practitioner session 5 goes deep on this.
Self-studyWhat a borrowed dataset must ship with3 min▶

Before you accept another team's episodes, ask for the embodiment as metadata, not as a paragraph in an email: robot and gripper, camera poses and which one is the canonical view, action space and unit, whether actions are absolute or relative, control frequency, and the normalisation statistics if they already trained on it. The identity bundle from practitioner session 1 is the template. If they cannot answer, the dataset is a research curiosity, not a training input.

Part 2 · covers coarse alignment and per-dataset normalisation

Coarse alignment: what was made common, and what was not

8 min live

The pooling recipe is deliberately coarse. Every dataset's action becomes a 7-dimensional end-effector action: x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw and gripper. Every dataset contributes one canonical camera view, resized to a common resolution. Each dataset's actions are normalised with its own statistics before training, and the model's outputs are de-normalised per embodiment on the way out. What is not aligned matters as much: coordinate frames, camera poses, and whether actions are positions or velocities.

LiveWhy normalise per dataset, not once for the pool3 min▶

A squared-error loss charges for the size of the mistake in the units of the data. If one dataset logs end-effector steps in metres (a typical step of 0.004) and another logs centimetres (a typical step of 0.4), the second dataset's errors are a hundred times larger in raw numbers and ten thousand times larger once squared. The optimiser spends almost all its effort on the centimetre dataset. Normalising the pool as a whole does not fix it, because the pooled mean and spread are still set by the larger numbers. Normalising each dataset with its own mean and standard deviation puts every dataset on the same scale, and keeping those statistics lets you turn a prediction back into each robot's units.

LiveThree runs worth doing in the calculator3 min▶
  • The default. Normalisation off, equal sizes: the borrowed dataset's mean squared step is about 10989 times Wrenfield's and takes 99.99 percent of the loss. Wrenfield's own data is effectively invisible to the optimiser.
  • Turn normalisation on. The ratio drops to 1 and each dataset carries half the loss. Same data, a fair hearing.
  • Now pick 10x with normalisation on. Scale is fixed, but the borrowed data still outnumbers yours and still takes most of the loss. Normalisation fixes units, not proportions. Sampling weights per dataset are the separate decision Part 3 sets up.
LiveReal world: what the authors chose not to align2 min▶
Real world

The Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) state that camera observations "still vary substantially" across datasets, in camera pose and properties, and that "we do not align the coordinate frames across datasets". Actions may be absolute or relative positions or velocities. Their argument is that a large enough model can learn some of these differences from the data itself. Whether that holds for your model size is exactly what Part 3 tests.

Self-studyStore the statistics like a schema2 min▶

Normalisation statistics are part of the model, not a training detail. Version them with the dataset they came from, store them next to the checkpoint, and refuse to deploy a checkpoint whose statistics file is missing. A policy whose output is de-normalised with another dataset's statistics will move with confidence and the wrong scale, and nothing in the loss curve will have warned you.

Part 3 · covers whether pooling helps, and the other route

Pooling helped the small and hurt the large

5 min live

The honest answer to "does pooling help?" is "for whom?". The same paper that pooled 60 datasets reports both outcomes, and a second large collection took the opposite approach entirely. Teach both; the literature has not settled which route wins.

LiveRead both halves of the result2 min▶

On domains with little data of their own, the pooled model RT-1-X had a mean success rate 50 percent higher than the original method trained on each dataset alone. On the large-scale datasets, the same model underfit and performed worse than the originals. The authors' reading is capacity: a model of that size cannot fit 22 embodiments' worth of variety, so the robot with plenty of its own data pays for everyone else's. The larger RT-2-X showed about 3x on emergent skills, which is the case for scaling the model with the pool.

Real world

DROID (Khazatsky et al. 2024) took the other route. Rather than pooling different bodies, it replicated the same hardware setup 18 times and had 50 collectors gather 76k demonstration trajectories across 564 scenes. Embodiment was held constant so that the variety went into scenes, objects and lighting instead. Neither paper settles which strategy is better in general; they answer different questions.

LiveQuestions to ask before pooling3 min▶
  • Frames. Which coordinate frame is each dataset's action in, and does +x point the same way? If not, what will the model learn from the disagreement?
  • Action type. Absolute position, relative position or velocity? Mixing them without a flag is mixing labels.
  • Camera. Which view is canonical for each dataset, and does it show the gripper?
  • Normalisation statistics. Computed per dataset, versioned, and shipped with the checkpoint?
  • Per-robot evaluation. Is success reported separately for each robot, including the one with the most data? A pooled average can hide a robot that got worse. Practitioner session 7 covers how few trials that comparison can rest on.
Self-studyWhat Wrenfield should do with the offer2 min▶

Wrenfield (constructed) has little data of its own, which is the side of the result where pooling helped. That is a reason to try, not a reason to trust. Train two policies, one on Wrenfield's data alone and one pooled with per-dataset normalisation, evaluate both on Wrenfield's robot under the same initial conditions, and keep the pooled one only if it wins there. If the partner's robot is also evaluated, report it separately. The decision rule is written before the trials, not after.

Build-along

Normalise, pool and de-normalise in numpy

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Python 3.10 or later with numpy. You build two constructed datasets of end-effector steps, one in metres and one in centimetres, measure how unequal they are, compute each one's mean and standard deviation, normalise and pool them, and then take one model output and turn it back into each robot's units. The figure shows the statistics you store and the round trip they make possible.

Build two constructed datasets in different units

Four hundred end-effector steps each, three axes. Wrenfield logs metres with a typical step of 0.004; the borrowed set logs centimetres with a typical step of 0.4. Physically they are the same size of motion; numerically they are a hundred times apart.

pool.py, part 1import numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(4) # constructed Wrenfield data # 400 end-effector steps (x, y, z) from each source wren = rng.normal(0.001, 0.004, size=(400, 3)) # Wrenfield logs metres borrowed = rng.normal(0.1, 0.4, size=(400, 3)) # borrowed set logs centimetres

Measure the imbalance, then the statistics

The mean squared step is what a squared-error loss is proportional to. Print the ratio, then each dataset's per-axis mean and standard deviation.

pool.py, part 2def msq(a): return float((a ** 2).sum(axis=1).mean()) # mean squared step size print("raw msq ratio, borrowed / wrenfield:", round(msq(borrowed) / msq(wren))) stats = {} for name, a in [("wrenfield", wren), ("borrowed", borrowed)]: stats[name] = (a.mean(axis=0), a.std(axis=0)) print(name, "mean", np.round(stats[name][0], 4), "std", np.round(stats[name][1], 4))

Normalise each dataset with its own statistics, then pool

Subtract the dataset's own mean and divide by its own standard deviation. After this, both datasets have the same scale and the pool's spread is 1 on every axis.

pool.py, part 3norm = {name: (a - stats[name][0]) / stats[name][1] for name, a in [("wrenfield", wren), ("borrowed", borrowed)]} print("normalised msq ratio:", round(msq(norm["borrowed"]) / msq(norm["wrenfield"]), 3)) pooled = np.vstack([norm["wrenfield"], norm["borrowed"]]) print("pooled shape:", pooled.shape, "pooled std:", np.round(pooled.std(axis=0), 3))

De-normalise one prediction back to each robot

Pretend the model output one normalised step. Multiply by each robot's own standard deviation and add its own mean. Then confirm the round trip on real data is exact.

pool.py, part 4z = np.array([0.5, -1.0, 0.2]) # one model output, normalised units for name, (m, s) in stats.items(): print(name, "step:", np.round(z * s + m, 4)) back = norm["wrenfield"] * stats["wrenfield"][1] + stats["wrenfield"][0] print("round trip exact:", np.allclose(back, wren))

Run it and read the output

Run python3 pool.py from a scratch folder. The output below is what the script printed when this page was written, with numpy's seeded generator; a different numpy version may print slightly different means.

Expected outputraw msq ratio, borrowed / wrenfield: 8940 wrenfield mean [0.0012 0.001 0.0006] std [0.004 0.0042 0.004 ] borrowed mean [0.0879 0.1023 0.1256] std [0.38 0.396 0.3763] normalised msq ratio: 1.0 pooled shape: (800, 3) pooled std: [1. 1. 1.] wrenfield step: [ 0.0032 -0.0032 0.0015] borrowed step: [ 0.278 -0.2937 0.2008] round trip exact: True

Extend it: break it on purpose

De-normalise the Wrenfield prediction with the borrowed dataset's statistics instead of its own, and print the result in metres. That number is the step the robot would take if the wrong statistics file shipped with the checkpoint. Then save stats to a JSON file named after the dataset version.

The habit to keep. The 8940 is the warning: two datasets describing the same size of motion differed by nearly four orders of magnitude in what a loss would see. Compute the ratio before you pool, normalise per dataset, and ship the statistics with the model.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why normalise each dataset with its own statistics rather than pooling raw actions?

Scale, not meaning: normalisation puts every dataset on the same footing for the loss and makes the round trip back to real units possible. It does nothing about frames.

The pooling authors state that they "do not align the coordinate frames across datasets". What does that risk?

"+x" on one robot may point where "+y" points on another. Normalisation rescales each axis; it cannot rotate one robot's frame into another's.

What did the Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) report for RT-1-X?

Both halves are in the same paper. Pooling helped robots with little data and hurt those with plenty, at that model size. Evaluate per robot before you trust a pooled average.

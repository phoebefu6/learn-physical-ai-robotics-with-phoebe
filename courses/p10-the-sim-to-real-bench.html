The sim-to-real bench

Everything from the practitioner track on one bench. Wrenfield Lab's grasp policy trains in your browser on simulated episodes with the randomization widths you choose, then faces a constructed real cell that is dimmer than the simulator, has its camera somewhere else, and a lens the simulator does not know about. You read the result the way session 7 taught: successes over trials, a Wilson interval, a sim-to-real gap and a per-condition scorecard. Then you over-randomize and watch both numbers fall, and you bump the real camera and watch the robot miss every parcel. Everything here is synthetic, declared on the widget, and computed in the page.

Wrenfield Lab (constructed) has a simulator, 400 simulated grasp episodes a run, twenty real demonstrations recorded by an operator, and fifty real trials of the afternoon's policy on the packing bench. The lead asks one question: is it ready? The bench below answers it the only honest way, by running every rung of the ladder on the same real trials, printing the interval next to each rate, and showing where in the cell the misses live.

LiveWhat is real and what is constructed3 min▶

Real: the mathematics. Ridge regression solved exactly, the Wilson (1927) score interval, Fisher's exact test, the idea of domain randomization from Tobin et al. (2017), and the habit of mixing a few real demonstrations into simulated training.

Constructed, and declared on the widget: Wrenfield, its robot, its cell, the way lighting bends what the camera sees (a bias of 5d + 5d squared centimetres, d being the lighting change), the real camera's offset, the 8 percent lens, the 1.0 cm grasp tolerance, and every trial. Nothing here is a claim about any real robot. The point is that in a constructed world the truth is known, so you can see what 50 trials do and do not tell you.

The figure is the constructed world the engine simulates. Three things stand between where a parcel is and where the camera says it is: the lighting, the camera's mounting, and the lens. Self-studyWhy the policy can learn to cancel the camera4 min▶

The camera's mounting offset adds the same amount to where the parcel appears and to where the table fiducial appears. If the simulator varies that offset, the regression sees the two readings move together and learns to subtract one from the other: at rung 3 the fiducial coefficient b2 is -0.994. Without camera randomization the fiducial never moves in simulation, its coefficient sits near zero (0.016 at rung 1), and every real offset passes straight into the grasp.

This is the mechanism behind Tobin et al. (2017) placing the simulated camera anywhere inside a 10 by 5 by 10 cm box: the model has to learn features that do not depend on exactly where the camera is. In their ablation, dropping camera randomization raised real error from 1.3 to 2.0 cm on objects alone.

Part 2 · covers the scorecard and where the misses live

A rate is a summary; the scorecard is the diagnosis

10 min live

An overall success rate tells you whether to worry. A breakdown by condition tells you what to fix. The bench splits the 50 real trials two ways: by lighting (33 dim trials below 0.85, 17 normal) and by where the parcel sat (29 near the centre, 21 near an edge). Each remaining failure has an address.

LiveReading the four columns5 min▶

Rung 1 fails in the dim. 17 of 33 dim trials against 12 of 17 normal. The simulator only ever rendered one brightness, so the policy has no idea that a darker image moves the apparent position.

Rung 2 fixes the dim, not the rest. Dim rises to 25 of 33; edge is still 14 of 21. The camera offset is still unhandled.

Rung 3 leaves one address. 30 of 33 dim, 16 of 17 normal, 29 of 29 centre, but 17 of 21 at the edge. A lens that magnifies by 8 percent moves a parcel at 9 cm by about 0.7 cm and a parcel at 1 cm by 0.08 cm. Against a 1.0 cm tolerance, only the edges notice.

Rung 4 closes it. Twenty real demonstrations carry the real lens, and the edge reads 20 of 21. No simulator width could have done that, because the simulator does not have the lens in it.

Self-studySmall cells, wide intervals4 min▶

Breaking 50 trials into conditions gives cells of 17 to 33. The scorecard prints a Wilson interval for each cell, and they are wide: 17 of 21 is 60 to 92 percent. A per-condition breakdown is a place to look, not a verdict per condition. Kress-Gazit et al. (2024) make the same point from real experiments: success depends heavily on the initial condition, so comparing policies on different initial conditions can produce a false conclusion. Match them, and report them.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

A lever that makes it worse, and a button that breaks it

10 min live

A bench that can only report good news is not a bench. Two controls make it report bad news, each for a reason a real team meets. The anti-lever over-randomizes: lighting from almost black to almost double, the camera anywhere within 12 cm. The break button moves the real camera 2 cm after calibration and changes nothing the policy reads about it.

LiveWhat the anti-lever teaches5 min▶

Press "Over-randomize everything". The simulator itself now scores 47.9 percent, because many of its episodes are so dark that the image is mostly noise. The real cell scores 19 of 50, worse than doing nothing at all (29 of 50). The scorecard says where: only 2 of 17 grasps succeed in normal light, the condition the real cell is best at.

The mechanism is capacity. Wrenfield's policy is a straight line in brightness, and the lighting effect is a curve. Fitted over a narrow range, a line is a good approximation of a curve; fitted over a huge range, the line is pulled by the extremes and misses the middle. Real policies have far more capacity than a line, but they have finite capacity and finite data, and the same trade applies: every training episode spent on conditions that never occur is not spent on the ones that do. Mehta et al. (2019) attribute "suboptimal, high-variance policies" to uniform randomization. How wide is right is a judgement made per robot, and it is checked on the real cell.

LiveWhat the break button teaches4 min▶

Press "Bump the real camera 2 cm". Rung 4, which scored 49 of 50, scores 0 of 50, and the true rate over 2,000 constructed trials falls from 0.951 to 0.025. Nothing in the model changed. The policy reads the fiducial position from the calibration it was given; the camera moved after that, so every grasp lands about 2 cm off against a 1.0 cm tolerance.

In real collections teams calibrate on a schedule and check it. DROID (2024) recorded an initial checkerboard calibration and later ran an automatic calibration with quality metrics across tens of thousands of scenes. The lesson for a data team: a calibration file is part of every episode's identity (practitioner session 2), and a silent change to it is a silent change to every row after it.

The habit to keep. A readiness note leads with successes over trials and the interval, then conditions, then what changed, then what breaks it. A note that leads with "98 percent" has buried the three facts a lead needs.

Check yourself

Three questions

After camera randomization Wrenfield's policy (constructed) scores 46 of 50, with 17 of 21 at the edges and 29 of 29 at the centre. What is the most useful next step?

The misses cluster at the edges, which points at a distortion that grows with distance from the centre: here the real lens. No simulator width contains it, so A cannot help; real demonstrations took the edge to 20 of 21.

Over-randomizing drops the simulator's own score to 47.9 percent and the real score to 19 of 50. Why does the simulator's score fall too?

A limited policy fitted across a huge range spends its accuracy on the extremes. The same seed and the same engine produce the 98 percent rows; only the widths changed.

With 20 trials, three policies score 18, 19 and 20 of 20. What can you conclude?

The Wilson intervals (70 to 97, 76 to 99, 84 to 100 percent) overlap almost completely. Practitioner session 7 shows how many trials, or what paired design, would separate them.

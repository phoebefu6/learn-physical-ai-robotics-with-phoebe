Anatomy of an episode

The unit of robot learning data is the episode: one attempt at a task, recorded from every camera and every joint on one clock, with a success flag at the end that a person decided. Tonight you take one apart. What is in it, which part is the label, what identifies it, and the five checks a data team runs before an episode is allowed anywhere near training. Wrenfield Lab and all its episodes are constructed; the research numbers are real and cited.

Part 0

Wrenfield's first export lands in the bucket

Wrenfield Lab (constructed) has recorded its first week of parcel picks. The export is a folder per episode: image files from three cameras, a table of joint readings and commands at 50 rows a second, and a small metadata file. The analyst's instinct is to flatten it into one table and start counting. Tonight is about why that loses the two things that matter most, the clock and the identity, and what to check first.

LiveWhy "one row per event" fails here4 min▶

A warehouse fact table has one row per thing that happened. An episode has hundreds of rows per second of the same thing happening, from several sources that do not tick together, and the only row that resembles a business event is the last one: did it work? Flatten it and you either lose the time structure the policy learns from, or you repeat the success flag on every row and pretend a 500-row episode is 500 labelled examples.

Real world

In the low-cost two-arm rig of Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023), episodes took 8 to 14 seconds and were recorded at 50 Hz, so each was 400 to 700 time steps. Each observation was the follower arms' joint positions plus images from four cameras. Fifty such episodes, about 10 to 20 minutes of data, were enough to train some tasks to 80 to 90 percent success. The episode, not the row, was the unit they counted.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Timestep. One tick of the control clock. At 50 Hz, every 0.02 seconds.
  • Observation. What the policy is given at a timestep: images, joint positions, sometimes gripper force.
  • Action. What the policy outputs at a timestep: usually target joint positions or an end-effector motion (practitioner session 5).
  • Outcome. The success flag and, ideally, the rule used to set it.
  • Metadata. Everything that says which robot, calibration, policy or operator, and scene produced the episode. It is the episode's identity.
Part 1 · covers observations, actions, the clock and the outcome

An episode is lanes on one clock

15 min live

Draw an episode the way the robot experienced it: time runs left to right, and each source is a lane. Cameras produce frames; encoders produce joint readings; the policy or the operator produces commands; the system logs events like "gripper closed". At the very end sits one judgement. Everything you train on is a slice of these lanes; everything you evaluate is the judgement.

LiveWhich column is the action?5 min▶

In imitation learning the action is the label: the model learns to output it from the observations. Getting the column right is not obvious. In the two-arm rig of Zhao et al. (2023), a person moves small "leader" arms and the robot's "follower" arms track them. The recorded action is the leader's joint positions, not the follower's. The authors explain why: the force the robot applies is implicit in the difference between the two, through the low-level controller. Record the follower's positions as the action and you have thrown that away.

The general rule for a data team: the action column must be the command that was sent, at the time it was sent, in the units the controller accepted. Practitioner session 5 covers the choices (absolute or delta, joint or end-effector) and why each needs its own normalisation.

LiveThe outcome is a judgement, so record the rule4 min▶

Someone decided whether each episode succeeded. In DROID (Khazatsky et al. 2024), a large collection of 76k demonstration trajectories gathered by 50 collectors over 12 months, collectors marked each episode as success or failure, failed episodes were excluded from policy training batches, and about 16k unsuccessful trajectories were released separately. The flag decided what the model was allowed to learn from.

So the flag needs its rule next to it: "parcel in tote, tote upright" is a different dataset from "parcel lifted". When the rule changes mid-collection, every episode after the change has a different label. Leader session 3 and practitioner session 7 come back to this.

Self-studyClocks: what we own and what we link3 min▶

Sensors sampling at different rates, timestamps that mean "captured" or "received", and joining streams with a tolerance are general time-series problems. They are taught in depth in learn IoT data. What is particular to robots is that the commands are in the same episode as the readings, so a lost or shifted step does not just blur a chart: it pairs an observation with the wrong action, and the policy learns the mismatch.

Part 2 · covers metadata as the episode's identity

An episode's identity is a bundle, not a key

8 min live

Two episodes are comparable only if you know what produced each one. An episode id is a name; the identity is the bundle behind it. Change any part of the bundle and the same pixels mean something different: a new calibration moves every coordinate, a new policy changes which situations get visited, a new operator changes the style of every demonstration.

LiveWhat the large collections had to standardise4 min▶

When the Open X-Embodiment collaboration (2023) pooled 60 datasets from 22 robot types and 21 institutions, it had to decide how to treat identity it could not reconcile. It chose one canonical camera view per dataset and normalised each dataset's actions, and its authors state plainly that camera observations "still vary substantially" across datasets and that they did "not align the coordinate frames". DROID (2024) took the opposite route: the same hardware setup replicated 18 times, with calibration recorded and later re-estimated automatically with quality metrics. Practitioner session 4 compares the two choices.

Self-studyA naming habit that pays off3 min▶

Keep the identity fields in the episode's own metadata, not in a folder name or a spreadsheet next to it. Folder names get renamed; spreadsheets drift. A loader that refuses an episode with a missing calibration id is cheaper than a month of training on data whose camera moved.

Part 3 · covers five checks before training

Five checks that catch most broken episodes

7 min live

Before an episode is used, five cheap checks: the clock has no holes, every camera is present and saw the robot, the action column is the command sent, the identity bundle is complete, and the outcome has a rule. The inspector below runs them on five constructed Wrenfield episodes. Each fails in a different way.

LiveWhy "teleoperator in camera view" is a check3 min▶

DROID's collection protocol was written to prevent mistakes such as "camera cannot see robot" and "teleoperator in camera view". The second one matters because a policy can learn to rely on whatever is in the picture. If a person's hand appears near the parcel in many successful demonstrations, the model may treat the hand as part of the scene it expects. Check 2 is not only "is the file there"; it is "does the frame show what the policy will see when nobody is helping".

Build-along

Checks 1 and 4 in pandas, on a constructed episode

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Python 3.10 or later with numpy and pandas. You build one constructed Wrenfield episode with a deliberate 12-step hole in its clock and a missing calibration id, then write the two checks that find them. The figure shows the gap check: the difference between consecutive timestamps should be one period, 0.02 seconds.

Build the constructed episode

Ten seconds at 50 Hz is 500 steps. Delete steps 200 to 211 to simulate a logging hiccup, and leave the calibration id empty in the metadata.

ep.py, part 1import numpy as np import pandas as pd rng = np.random.default_rng(7) # constructed Wrenfield episode hz, seconds = 50, 10 t = np.arange(hz * seconds) / hz # 500 timesteps, 0.02 s apart t = np.delete(t, np.arange(200, 212)) # 12 steps lost in logging steps = pd.DataFrame({ "t": t, "joint_0": np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.01, len(t))), "target_0": np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.01, len(t))), "cam_wrist_frame": np.arange(len(t)), }) meta = {"episode_id": "wf-000412", "robot_id": "wf-arm-2", "calibration_id": None, "policy": "teleop:op-07", "success": True, "success_rule": "parcel in tote, tote upright"}

Check 1: the clock has no holes

Difference consecutive timestamps, round away floating-point noise, and count gaps over 1.5 periods.

ep.py, part 2gaps = steps["t"].diff().round(3) print("steps:", len(steps)) print("largest gap (s):", gaps.max()) print("gaps over 1.5 periods:", int((gaps > 1.5 / hz).sum()))

Check 4: the identity is complete

List every metadata field that is empty. In a real loader this list decides quarantine.

Extend it: check 5

Add a line that fails the episode if success is set but success_rule is empty. Then set the rule to None and confirm your check fires.

The habit to keep. Checks run before training and send failures to quarantine with a reason. The 488 is a warning in itself: an episode that should have 500 steps and has 488 has told you something before you looked at a single image.

Check yourself

Three questions

In the teleoperation rig of Zhao et al. (2023), which joint positions were recorded as the action, and why?

The follower's positions are an observation. The action is the command, and in that rig the command is the leader's position; recording the follower's would lose the force information.

A constructed Wrenfield episode at 50 Hz has 488 steps over 10 seconds and one gap of 0.26 seconds. What is the right action?

Interpolating invents commands nobody sent. Training on it pairs observations with the wrong next action. Quarantine keeps the evidence and the decision visible.

Why does DROID's protocol treat "teleoperator in camera view" as a mistake?

The training frames should show what the policy will see at run time. A person in view during demonstrations is a feature that disappears at deployment.

Cheat sheet

The unit. An episode: lanes on one clock plus one outcome.
The label for learning. The action lane: the command sent, when it was sent.
The label for evaluation. The success flag, with its rule, versioned.
The identity. Robot, calibration, policy or operator, scene, task rule, software.
Five checks. Clock, cameras, action, identity, outcome.
Quarantine. Failures keep their reason; nothing is silently dropped.
The count. 50 Hz for 10 seconds is 500 steps; 488 is a finding.
The frame. Train on what the robot will see alone: no helping hands in view.

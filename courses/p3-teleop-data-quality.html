Teleop data quality

Part 0

Wrenfield wants a clean demonstration set

Wrenfield Lab (constructed) has three operators collecting parcel-pick demonstrations. A week in, someone proposes the obvious cleanup: drop the slow takes, drop the ones with long pauses, drop anything with a re-grasp. It sounds like quality control. Tonight you find out what those three rules remove, and it is not mostly mistakes.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Why the variation between demonstrations is information, and what happened in one published rig when the model was not allowed to represent it. The four quality problems you can see in a teleoperation log, and the two you cannot. A filter widget that shows how a sensible-looking cut-off empties the dim and edge conditions from a constructed table. A per-operator coverage grid with its empty cell. And a pandas script that counts, by condition, what each filter removes.

LiveA demonstration is expensive, so every one you drop costs3 min▶

A filter feels free because deleting a row is free. Collecting it was not. Every episode you remove is an hour of someone's attention divided by however many takes fit in it, and if the filter removes the wrong ones, the cost is paid twice: once in collection, again in a policy that never saw the situations it fails on.

Real world

Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) collected 50 demonstrations per task on their low-cost two-arm rig (100 for one harder task). That was about 10 to 20 minutes of recorded data per task, but 30 to 60 minutes of wall-clock time, because of resets between takes and mistakes by the teleoperator. Roughly half or more of the session was spent on things that never became training data.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Teleoperation. A person drives the robot, here by moving leader arms the robot's follower arms copy (practitioner session 1).
  • Take. One attempt by an operator; it becomes an episode when it is recorded.
  • Pause fraction. The share of an episode's time where the commanded motion is near zero. A Wrenfield house metric, constructed.
  • Re-grasp. The gripper opens and closes again on the same object within one episode.
  • Coverage. Which conditions (light, object position, operator) the set actually contains, counted, not assumed.
Part 1 · covers why demonstrations vary by design

Demonstrations vary, and the average does nothing

8 min live

Ask one person to hand a parcel from the left gripper to the right gripper fifty times and you get fifty slightly different handovers. Each one works. A model that learns the average of them learns a handover that nobody performed, and it can land where no gripper is waiting. Variation in a demonstration set is not noise to be scrubbed; some of it is the task.

LiveWhat happened when the model could not represent the spread4 min▶

Zhao et al. (2023) describe human demonstrations as "inherently stochastic", even from a single person: in their bimanual tasks the handover position differs every episode. Their model, ACT, is trained with a CVAE objective, a component that lets it represent several valid ways of doing the task instead of one blurred compromise.

Real world

In their ablation on human demonstration data, removing the CVAE objective dropped success from 35.3 percent to 2 percent. Same data, same tasks; the only change was whether the model was allowed to keep the variation. The data team's lesson: the spread between demonstrations is part of the signal, so a cleanup that "reduces variance" can remove exactly what the model needed.

LiveVariation you want: coverage2 min▶

Different handover heights, different approach angles, the parcel at the centre and at the edge, bright and dim light, three operators with three styles. Each is a situation the policy will meet when nobody is driving. Keep it, count it, and make sure it is spread across conditions rather than piled in one corner.

LiveVariation you do not want: mistakes2 min▶

An operator who freezes to read a message, a knocked parcel that is pushed back by hand, a grasp that slips because the operator rushed, a hand in the camera frame. These are behaviours the policy would copy and conditions it will never see alone. Remove them, but by reason, with the reason recorded, not by a threshold that also catches the hard conditions.

Part 2 · covers what quality problems look like in the log

Reading quality off the log, and where filters go wrong

7 min live

You rarely get to watch every take. What you get is the log: commands at 50 rows a second, gripper events, camera frames and a success flag somebody set. Four problems are visible there: long stretches of near-zero motion, re-grasps, an operator in the frame, and a camera that cannot see the robot. Two are not: why the operator paused, and whether the success flag was set by a consistent rule.

LiveThe four you can see, and the protocol that prevents two3 min▶
  • Pauses. Long stretches of near-zero commanded motion. Could be hesitation, could be an operator waiting for a camera to refocus in dim light.
  • Re-grasps. Open, re-approach, close. Could be a sloppy approach, could be a parcel at the edge of the reach that needs two tries for anyone.
  • Operator in camera view. A hand or sleeve in the frame. Always a problem: it will not be there at run time.
  • Camera cannot see the robot. A frame the policy cannot learn from. Always a problem.
Real world

DROID (Khazatsky et al. 2024), 76k demonstration trajectories from 50 collectors over 12 months, wrote its collection protocol to prevent "camera cannot see robot" and "teleoperator in camera view" before they reached the data. Collectors marked each episode success or failure; failed episodes were excluded from policy training, and about 16k unsuccessful trajectories were released separately rather than thrown away. Note which way the rules point: the last two problems are prevented at collection, not filtered afterwards by a proxy.

LiveWhy the filters remove the hard conditions2 min▶

With all three rules on, the widget keeps 6 of 12 episodes, and none of the 5 dim-light or 4 edge episodes survive. Only one removed take, wf-p3-10, is a plain mistake: a long pause in normal light with the parcel at the centre. Every other removal is an operator doing a harder thing more slowly. Duration, pauses and re-grasps are not measures of quality; they are measures of difficulty with some quality mixed in. A filter on a difficulty proxy is a filter on difficulty.

Speed is not a free proxy either. In the user study of Zhao et al. (2023), six participants teleoperating at 5 Hz instead of 50 Hz took 62 percent longer. If your rig's frame rate or latency changed mid-collection, a duration filter removes the episodes recorded on the slow setup, not the bad ones.

Self-studyThe success flag is also a quality field2 min▶

The collector sets the flag. If one operator marks "parcel lifted" as success and another waits for "parcel in tote", the flag means two things. Before using it as a filter, check success rate per operator against the written rule (practitioner session 1 asks for the rule to be stored next to the flag). A failed episode is not a quality problem; it is a labelled outcome. Keep it, with its reason, in its own table.

Part 3 · covers per-operator effects

The operator is a hidden variable

5 min live

Each operator brings a style (how fast, how they approach, where they hand over) and, less obviously, a set of conditions: the scenes they happened to set up, the shifts they worked, the parcels they placed. If one operator did most of the hard conditions, then "operator B is slow" and "dim light is slow" are the same column. Report per operator, rotate operators across conditions, and the two separate.

LiveThree habits that make the operator visible3 min▶
  • Report per operator. Episode count, success count, mean pause fraction and how many hard-condition episodes, one row each. The build-along prints exactly this table.
  • Rotate operators across conditions. Write the schedule as a grid like the one above and fill it deliberately. An empty cell is a planning decision, not an accident to discover after training.
  • Keep failures with a reason. A failure table (episode, operator, condition, reason) tells you whether a condition is hard for everyone or for one person. Deleting failures deletes that evidence.
Real world

DROID's 76k trajectories came from 50 data collectors across 564 scenes, on the same hardware setup replicated 18 times. At that scale the operator stops being a footnote and becomes a column like scene and calibration: every comparison across the set quietly compares people too, unless the collection plan spread them across conditions.

Self-studyWhat the literature does not settle2 min▶

How much operator style helps (more modes for the model to represent) or hurts (inconsistent strategies the model cannot reconcile) is not settled by the sources read for this session. Zhao et al. (2023) show that a model which can represent variation does far better on human data than one that cannot; they do not measure how many operators is too many. Treat operator diversity as a variable you report and test, not one you assume.

Build-along

Count what each filter removes, by condition

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Python 3.10 or later with pandas. You build the same twelve constructed Wrenfield episodes as the widget, summarise them per operator, apply the three filter rules one at a time and count what each removes by condition, then compare the naive set with a reasoned one: drop only pauses that the condition does not explain, and move failures to their own table.

Build the constructed table

Twelve episodes, four per operator. Hard means dim light or the parcel at the edge of reach. These are the same values as the widget's table.

teleop.py, part 1import pandas as pd # 12 constructed Wrenfield episodes, 3 operators eps = pd.DataFrame({ "episode": [f"wf-p3-{i:02d}" for i in range(1, 13)], "operator": ["A"] * 4 + ["B"] * 4 + ["C"] * 4, "duration_s": [9.8, 11.2, 15.6, 10.4, 12.9, 14.8, 10.1, 14.6, 8.9, 16.2, 9.5, 13.1], "pause_frac": [0.08, 0.12, 0.34, 0.10, 0.22, 0.31, 0.09, 0.27, 0.05, 0.41, 0.07, 0.18], "regrasps": [0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 0, 1], "success": [True, True, True, False, True, True, True, True, True, True, True, False], "light": ["normal", "normal", "dim", "normal", "dim", "dim", "normal", "dim", "normal", "normal", "normal", "dim"], "edge": [False, False, True, False, False, True, False, True, False, False, False, True], }) eps["hard"] = (eps["light"] == "dim") | eps["edge"]

Summarise per operator

One row per operator: episodes, successes, mean pause fraction and how many hard-condition episodes they recorded. Read the pause column next to the hard column before judging anyone.

teleop.py, part 2print(eps.groupby("operator").agg( episodes=("episode", "size"), successes=("success", "sum"), mean_pause=("pause_frac", "mean"), hard=("hard", "sum"), ).round(3))

Apply each rule alone and count what it removes

Each rule is a boolean mask of episodes to drop. Count the removed episodes by condition; the last column is the only place a plain mistake can hide.

teleop.py, part 3rules = { "pause over 0.3": eps["pause_frac"] > 0.3, "duration over 14 s": eps["duration_s"] > 14, "any re-grasp": eps["regrasps"] > 0, } print() for name, drop in rules.items(): gone = eps[drop] print(f"{name:20s} removes {len(gone)}: dim {int((gone.light == 'dim').sum())}, " f"edge {int(gone.edge.sum())}, normal-and-centre {int((~gone.hard).sum())}")

Naive versus reasoned

Naive drops anything any rule catches. Reasoned drops a long pause only when the condition does not explain it, and moves failures to their own table instead of deleting them.

teleop.py, part 4naive = eps[~(rules["pause over 0.3"] | rules["duration over 14 s"] | rules["any re-grasp"])] unexplained_pause = (eps["pause_frac"] > 0.3) & ~eps["hard"] failures = eps[~eps["success"]] # kept, with a reason, in their own table reasoned = eps[~unexplained_pause & eps["success"]] def share(df): return f"{len(df)} episodes, dim {int((df.light == 'dim').sum())}, edge {int(df.edge.sum())}, hard share {df.hard.mean():.0%}" print() print("all :", share(eps)) print("naive :", share(naive)) print("reasoned:", share(reasoned)) print("failure table:", list(failures["episode"]))

Extend it: a reason column

Add a reason column to the failure table (for example "slip at edge", "dropped in tote") and print failures grouped by condition. Then add a crosstab of operator against light and edge, and find the empty cell from Part 3 in your own output.

The habit to keep. Before any filter runs, print what it removes by condition. A rule that removes zero normal-and-centre episodes, like "any re-grasp" here, is not a quality filter at all: it is a difficulty filter with a quality name.

Check yourself

Three questions

In Zhao et al. (2023), removing the CVAE objective when training on human demonstrations dropped success from 35.3 percent to 2 percent. What does that say about averaging demonstrations?

The same data worked with the CVAE objective. What failed was collapsing several valid ways of doing the task into one average, like a handover that ends where no gripper waits.

Why is "drop episodes longer than 14 seconds" a biased filter on Wrenfield's constructed table?

A slow take is often a hard take. Zhao et al. (2023) also found 5 Hz teleoperation took 62 percent longer than 50 Hz, so duration can track the rig as well as the operator.

What should a data team do with failed episodes?

A failure is a labelled outcome. Kept with operator, condition and reason, it shows which conditions are hard for everyone and feeds evaluation and later rounds (practitioner session 8).

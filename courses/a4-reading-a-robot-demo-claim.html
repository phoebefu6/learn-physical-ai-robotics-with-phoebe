Reading a robot demo claim

Most robot claims reach a leader as a video before they reach anyone as a table. A video is honest about one thing: on one occasion, something happened. It is silent about how fast it was played, what was cut, whether a person was driving, how many tries it took and who decided it counted. Tonight is how to read those silences and ask for them back. Every example clip belongs to Wrenfield Lab, the parcel-packing team in every session, and is constructed: no real company's video or product is described anywhere. The research numbers are real and cited.

Part 0

Wrenfield Lab, the morning a clip lands in the board deck

Wrenfield Lab is constructed. Its robotics lead has cut a 30-second clip for the quarterly review: the two-arm robot lifts a soft parcel, turns it, and drops it neatly into a tote. The board loves it. The analyst from the warehouse is asked one question: "so can we say it packs soft parcels now?" She has the clip and nothing else. Tonight is the list of things she needs before she can answer, and the polite way to ask for them.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The five things a robot video can hide: playback speed, cuts, an operator off camera, resets between attempts, and the best take of many. The arithmetic of the best take: if a policy succeeds one time in five, ten tries produce at least one usable clip 89.3 percent of the time, so a single clip says only that the success rate is above zero. LiveWhy a demo is not a result3 min▶

A warehouse dashboard shows every order, the good and the bad, because the system logs them all. A demo video shows what someone chose to show. That is not dishonest in itself: demos exist to show what is possible, and a team with something new to show should show it. The mistake is on the viewer's side, when a clip of something possible is read as a measure of how often it happens.

The fix is not suspicion. It is a short list of questions whose answers turn a clip back into a claim: how fast, what was cut, who was driving, how many tries, and judged by what rule.

Real world

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024), writing on best practice for evaluating robot policies, observe that papers usually report a success rate with little about the number of runs, the initial conditions, the success criteria or any statistical analysis. If peer-reviewed papers leave those out, a video made for an audience will leave out more. The questions below are the same ones they ask of papers, pointed at a clip.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Playback speed. How fast the clip is played compared with real time. "4x" means four seconds of the robot's life pass in one second of video.
  • Cut. An edit that removes time: a reset, a failed attempt, a pause. A clip with no cuts is one continuous take.
  • Teleoperation. A person drives the robot, often by moving a leader arm that the robot copies, in the room or remotely. The normal way to collect demonstrations (leader session 2).
  • Autonomous. The policy chose every action in the attempt shown, with no human input. A claim that needs saying out loud, and backing with how it was checked.
  • Reset. A person puts the scene back to a starting state between attempts. Every real trial needs one; none appear in a polished clip.
  • Take. One recorded attempt. The best take of many is the one that makes the video.
  • Disclosure card. Six fields that travel with a clip: speed, cuts, autonomous or teleoperated, attempts shown of attempts made, success rule, conditions.
Part 1 · covers playback speed, cuts, hidden teleoperation, resets and selection

Thirty seconds of video, two minutes of robot

10 min live

The clearest way to read a demo is to draw what was recorded underneath what was shown. Wrenfield's constructed clip is 30 seconds long. The robot's real session behind it was 120 seconds, and four kinds of editing sit between the two: parts were cut, one part was sped up four times, the hardest part was driven by a person who is not in frame, and the take itself was chosen from several. None of that is visible in the clip.

LiveFive things a clip can hide, and the question for each5 min▶

Playback speed. A sped-up clip makes a slow, careful policy look fluent. Speeding up is a reasonable way to keep a video short; the norm is that the factor stays on screen ("4x") for every sped-up second. Question: was any part played faster than real time, and is that labelled?

Cuts. A cut removes time, and time is where failures live: a dropped parcel, a hesitation, a retry. Question: is this one continuous take, and if not, what was removed?

Hidden teleoperation. A person can drive the robot from just off camera or from another room. The motion looks identical to autonomy, because the robot's arm is doing the same thing either way. Question: did the policy choose every action in what is shown, and how do you know?

Resets. Between attempts somebody tidies the table, straightens the parcel and moves the tote back. Question: what did the scene look like at the start, and who arranged it?

The best take of many. The clip is one attempt, and it was chosen. Question: how many attempts were made to get this one? Part 2 is what that number does to the meaning of the clip.

These are the video versions of the gaps Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) find in papers: the number of runs, the initial conditions and the success criteria, left unreported.

LiveTeleoperation is legitimate; unlabelled teleoperation is not3 min▶

Driving a robot by hand is not a trick. It is how the training data is made: in leader session 2, every demonstration is a person moving a leader arm while the robot copies. A clip of a teleoperated robot doing something delicate is honest evidence that the hardware can do it and that the data can be collected.

It is not evidence that a policy can do it. Those are different claims, and the second is the one a leader usually wants. So a claim of autonomy has to say "autonomous" in words, and say how that was checked: for example, that the operator station was switched off or absent, or that the episode log shows no human commands during the attempt. Absent that sentence, treat the clip as "possibly teleoperated", not as a lie and not as autonomy.

Real world

Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) record the leader arm's joint positions as the action in every demonstration, because that is what the person moved. Their 50 demonstrations per task were 10 to 20 minutes of data and took 30 to 60 minutes of wall-clock time, because of resets and operator mistakes. A polished clip of any one of those demonstrations would show a perfect, fluent episode, and would show none of the other half hour.

Self-studyWhy the pixels cannot settle it4 min▶

A warehouse analyst's instinct is to inspect the evidence harder: slow it down, look for the operator's shadow, count frames. It rarely works. A teleoperated arm and an autonomous one produce the same motion; the difference is upstream of the motors, in which program sent the commands. A sped-up clip can be detected by things that move unnaturally fast, but a clip of a slow, smooth arm at 2x looks like a fast, smooth arm at 1x.

The evidence that settles it lives in the episode log, not the video: the timestamps of every command, which source sent them, and the list of every attempt with its outcome (practitioner session 1 is what an episode records). Ask for the log, or for a written statement drawn from it. That is a request a team with a real result can meet in an afternoon.

Part 2 · covers the best take of many, as arithmetic

One good clip from ten tries proves the rate is above zero

8 min live

Suppose Wrenfield's soft-parcel policy truly succeeds one time in five. That is a constructed rate, chosen to make the arithmetic easy to see. The team films ten attempts and keeps the best. How often will they have a clean success to show? The answer is "almost always", and that is the whole problem: a single success on video is what a weak policy and a strong one both produce.

Live Set a true success rate per attempt and a number of takes. The page computes, in your browser, the chance that at least one take succeeds, which is the chance the team has a clip to show. Arithmetic on a constructed rate: the defaults, 0.2 and 10 takes, are Wrenfield's invented example, and your own inputs give your own numbers.

...

Formula: P(at least one success) = 1 - (1 - p)^N, assuming takes are independent and each succeeds with the same rate p. Expected successes = N x p. Constructed inputs; nothing here describes a real robot.
LiveReading the widget at its defaults3 min▶

At a true rate of 0.2 and 10 takes, the chance of at least one success is 1 - 0.8^10 = 0.893. The expected number of successes is 2. The chance of having nothing at all to show is 0.107, about one shoot in nine.

Now push the rate down. Set the widget to 0.05, a policy that succeeds one time in twenty, and read how often ten takes still give the team a clip; then raise the takes. A clip is the event "at least one", and "at least one" is easy to reach. The only reading a single successful clip fully supports is: the success rate is above zero, under the conditions filmed.

That is still worth something. Above zero is not nothing when a task used to be impossible. It is just a different claim from "it works", and the two get confused in every board pack that contains a video.

LiveWhat to ask for instead of a better clip3 min▶

All the attempts. Not the best one, not a highlights reel: the full list, each with its outcome, including the ones that were stopped early.

k of n. The count of successes out of the count of attempts, written as a fraction, never as a percentage alone.

The interval. Put k of n through the Wilson interval from leader session 3. Two of ten and two hundred of a thousand are the same rate and very different evidence; the interval shows which one you have.

Real world

Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) evaluated each real task with 25 trials and reported success out of those trials. That pairing of a clip with a count is the norm worth asking for: the clip shows what the behaviour looks like, and the k of n shows how often it happens.

Self-studyWhere the simple formula is too kind, and too harsh4 min▶

The formula assumes every take is independent and has the same chance. Real shoots break both. The team tunes the scene between takes, so later takes may be easier than earlier ones, and "the best of ten" becomes "the best of ten, each a little more arranged". That makes a clip even less informative than the arithmetic says.

It can also go the other way. If a team states up front "we filmed one attempt and this is it", the selection problem disappears and a single success means more, though a sample of one is still a sample of one. Which is why the number of takes made is one of the six fields in Part 3, not a detail.

Part 3 · covers how a demo's success was judged, and the disclosure card

Who decided it counted, and was it a fair test

7 min live

Even with every attempt on the table, a rate depends on the rule that turned each attempt into a tick or a cross, on who applied it, and on how the attempts were set up. Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) give a short list of practices that make a robot result checkable. The same list, plus the editing questions from Part 1, fits on one card that can travel with any clip.

LiveFour practices that make a result checkable4 min▶

The designer writes the rule; someone else judges. Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) ask for "a clear, detailed, and unambiguous definition of success", written by the person who designed the behaviour, and applied by an evaluator who is not that person. The builder knows which wobble was "nearly fine"; the judge should not have to guess.

Interleave the policies, blind to the evaluator. If two versions are compared, run their attempts mixed together so the judge does not know which policy is on the arm.

One session for all policies. Lighting, room layout and the people in it drift between days. Evaluating every policy in the same session keeps that drift out of the comparison.

Matched initial conditions. Start every attempt from the same arrangement, using image overlays or markings on the table, so a policy is not helped by an easy start.

Real world

The same paper reports re-running one policy from the same initial conditions after a lab was reorganized: success fell from 13 of 20 to 0 of 8. The policy had not changed; the room had. A demo filmed in one room on one day is evidence about that room on that day.

LiveEven "human speed" depends on the rig2 min▶

Demos are often compared with a person: "it packs nearly as fast as a human". Which human, through which controls? Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) ran a small user study with 6 participants on their teleoperation rig. At 5 Hz instead of 50 Hz, teleoperation took 62 percent longer: a zip-tie task went from 20 seconds to 33, a cup task from 10 seconds to 16.

So the human baseline is itself a measurement with conditions. If a clip compares a robot with a person teleoperating a robot, ask about the rig as well as the policy.

Self-studyWhere people disagree about demos4 min▶

Demos are not only evidence. They are also how teams recruit, raise attention and show a direction, and people disagree about how much disclosure a marketing clip owes its viewers. Some argue that any video without its attempt count misleads; others that a clearly promotional clip is understood as a highlight and should not be held to a paper's standard.

The working position for a leader does not need to settle that. Treat every demo as evidence of possibility, not of rate. When a decision depends on the rate, ask for the six fields and the k of n; when it does not, enjoy the video. And when general-purpose robots will match a polished demo outside a controlled cell is contested; no page here gives a date.

Build-along

Score two clips and write one question for each

★ 17 min · everyone builds

No code. Below are two constructed Wrenfield clips, described in words because the point is what a clip does and does not tell you. Score each against the six-field disclosure card from Part 3, then write the single question you would send back to the team. One good question beats six; pick the field whose answer would most change the decision.

Draw the six-field card twice

Six rows: playback speed, cuts, autonomous or teleoperated, attempts shown of attempts made, success rule and who judged it, conditions. One copy for each clip. A field counts as filled only if the clip or its caption answers it; your guess does not count.

Score Clip A

Constructed: a single uncut take, played at 4x with "4x" in the corner, captioned "autonomous, episode log attached, 7 of 10 attempts succeeded". Fill each field from that description. Notice that a sped-up clip can still score well: the speed is disclosed, so it is a presentation choice, not a hiding place.

Score Clip B

Constructed: real-time footage, with two visible jumps where the scene changes, one successful pack, no caption beyond "soft parcels, packed". Fill each field. Most will be blank, and "teleoperated" is not mentioned either way.

Read the two scores as claims

Clip A supports a rate: 7 of 10, which leader session 3's widget turns into an interval, but it does not yet say what counted as a success or under which conditions. Clip B supports only "possible, at least once, by someone or something". Write one sentence for each starting "This clip shows that...".

Pick the one blank that matters most

For each clip, ask which unanswered field would most change your decision if it came back badly. For Clip A it is the success rule: a loose rule quietly turns near-misses into successes, and the 7 moves with it. For Clip B it is autonomy, because a teleoperated clip is not evidence about a policy at all.

Write the one question to send back

One line, polite, specific, answerable from the episode log in an afternoon. Compare yours with the figure. Then add the request that should travel with every future clip: "please attach the six fields and all attempts as k of n".

The habit to keep. Read a demo as evidence that something is possible, never as a rate. Before it goes in a board pack, attach the six fields: speed, cuts, autonomous or teleoperated, attempts shown of attempts made, success rule and judge, conditions. Every blank goes on the slide as a blank.

Check yourself

Three questions

A constructed policy truly succeeds 20 percent of the time. The team films 10 independent takes and shows the best one. How likely are they to have a success to show?

The clip needs only one success among ten. The chance of ten misses in a row is 0.8^10 = 0.107, so at least one success happens 89.3 percent of the time. Two is the expected count, not a guarantee: about one shoot in nine produces nothing.

A clip is captioned "autonomous". What should that word be backed by?

A teleoperated arm and an autonomous one make the same motion, so the pixels cannot settle it. Autonomy is a claim about where the commands came from, and the evidence is in the log. Teleoperation for data collection is legitimate; it is just a different claim.

Why should the person who built a policy not be the one who decides which attempts succeeded?

Kress-Gazit et al. (2024) recommend the designer write a clear, detailed, unambiguous definition of success and that someone else apply it. The point is a count anyone could reproduce, not a guaranteed lower number.

Before session 5

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one video and on your own reporting.

Score one public robot video against the six fields

Pick any robot video you have seen shared, from any source. Fill the six fields from what the video and its caption actually say, marking every blank. Do not judge the team or name it anywhere; the exercise is noticing which fields are routinely missing.

Find the "best take" in your own work

Any screenshot, chart or example you have shown a leadership audience because it looked best: one good customer story, one clean week, one model output. Write down how many candidates it was picked from, and what the k of n would have been. Leader session 5 asks why habits like this, harmless in a warehouse report, cost more when an arm moves.

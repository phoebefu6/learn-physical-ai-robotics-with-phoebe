Coordinate frames and calibration

The camera sees a parcel in its own coordinates; the arm can only move in its own. Between them sits a transform that someone measured, and every grasp depends on it being right. Tonight you name the four frames in a pick, learn what extrinsics and intrinsics are in plain words, compute what one degree of camera error costs at the table, and see why the calibration id belongs in every episode's identity. Wrenfield Lab, its bench, its calibration ids and its 1.0 cm grasp tolerance are constructed; the research numbers are real and cited.

LiveWhy "x = 0.30" means nothing on its own3 min▶

A number with a unit is still not a position. "x = 0.30 m" from where, along which direction? In a warehouse table a column has one meaning. In a robot episode the same three numbers can be measured from the camera, from the base of the arm, from the gripper, or from the parcel itself, and each answer is correct in its own frame. A dataset that does not say which frame a column is in has not finished recording the column.

Real world

When the Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) pooled 60 datasets from 22 robot embodiments, it mapped every action to a common 7-dimensional end-effector format, and then said plainly what it could not do: "we do not align the coordinate frames across datasets". Its authors add that "the same action vector may induce very different motions for different robots". The format matched; the frames did not. That is a stated limitation of one of the largest pooled robot datasets, and it is the subject of tonight.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Frame. An origin and a set of directions. Every position is written relative to one.
  • Transform. A rotation plus a translation that rewrites a point from one frame into another.
  • Extrinsics. Where a camera sits and which way it points, relative to something else (usually the robot base). A transform.
  • Intrinsics. What happens inside the camera: focal length, image centre, lens distortion. How a point in front of the lens becomes a pixel.
  • Calibration. The measurement of those numbers, done at a time, with a method, and given an id.
Part 1 · covers the four frames, transforms, extrinsics and intrinsics

Four frames, one parcel, two addresses

12 min live

Look down on Wrenfield's bench. The arm is bolted to the table at its base. A camera on a tripod at the far end looks back toward the arm. The gripper is wherever the arm last put it. The parcel sits between them. Each of the four has its own frame, and the parcel has a different address in each.

LiveA transform is a rotation, then a shift4 min▶

To move the parcel's address from the camera frame to the base frame, turn the camera's directions to line up with the base's, then add where the camera sits. On Wrenfield's bench (constructed) the camera faces back toward the arm, so its x axis points the opposite way to the base's: a rotation of 180 degrees. It sits 0.90 m along and 0.20 m across from the base. The parcel at (0.60, 0.00) m in front of the lens becomes (0.30, 0.20) m from the base. The build-along does this in three lines of numpy.

Real benches are three-dimensional, so the rotation has three angles and the shift three numbers, but the idea does not change. What matters for a data team is that the transform is a measured quantity with an error, not a constant of nature.

LiveExtrinsics and intrinsics, in plain words4 min▶

Extrinsics are the camera's place in the world: the marigold arrow. Lean on the tripod and they change. Intrinsics are the camera's own optics: how a point in front of the lens lands on a pixel, including lens distortion that bends straight lines near the edge of the image. Swap the lens or change the zoom and they change. Both are estimated, both drift or jump, and both decide where the robot thinks things are.

For a data team the split matters because they fail differently. A wrong extrinsic shifts everything by about the same amount. A wrong intrinsic hurts some parts of the image more than others, usually the edges. Session 10's bench has a lens effect of exactly that kind: the real lens only costs grasps near the edge of the table.

LiveWhy every action and observation must say its frame4 min▶

An action column of "target x, y, z" is ambiguous until it says base frame or gripper frame, and absolute or relative. Open X-Embodiment (2023) records that actions across its pooled datasets may be absolute or relative positions or velocities, and that frames were not aligned. When a model learns from such a mix, the same numbers mean different motions on different robots. Practitioner session 4 asks whether mixing still helps, and the answer is contested; practitioner session 5 covers how to store actions so the question can be asked at all.

The norm: every position-like column carries its frame in its name or its schema (parcel_xy_base, target_pose_ee), and every episode carries the calibration that links the frames.

Part 2 · covers calibration error and what one degree costs

What one degree costs at the table

8 min live

A small angle at the camera becomes a large distance at the table, and it grows with how far away the table is. The arithmetic is one line: the apparent point moves by the working distance times the tangent of the angle. At 60 cm, one degree of yaw is 600 x tan(1 degree) = 10.47 mm, about 1.05 cm. Wrenfield's grasp tolerance (constructed) is 1.0 cm. One degree spends all of it.

Live Computed in this page: apparent point error = working distance x tan(camera yaw error) + lateral mount offset. The 1.0 cm grasp tolerance is Wrenfield's and constructed. At the default settings it prints the derived 10.47 mm.

...

Worst case: the offset is added in the same direction as the yaw error. A real error has three angles and three shifts, and some of it may cancel; your bench's numbers will differ. Constructed tolerance, derived geometry.
LiveThree runs worth doing in the calculator3 min▶
  • The default. 1 degree at 60 cm prints 10.47 mm and the largest yaw the tolerance allows at that distance, 0.95 degrees. The budget is under a degree.
  • Move the camera closer. Halve the distance and the same degree costs half as much. Distance is a design choice with a calibration price.
  • Add a small mount offset. Half a centimetre of slip in the bracket eats half the tolerance before any angle is involved.
LiveReal world: two ways teams handled the camera4 min▶
Real world

Tobin et al. (2017) trained an object detector only on simulated images and did not try to know the camera exactly. Each simulated sample placed the camera somewhere within a 10 x 5 x 10 cm box around a hand-placed point that approximately matched the real camera, offset the viewing angle by up to 0.1 rad and scaled the field of view by up to 5%. The detector learned to tolerate camera error rather than depend on a perfect calibration. Practitioner session 6 covers that approach in full.

DROID (Khazatsky et al. 2024) took the measuring route. The same hardware setup was replicated 18 times, each with two exterior cameras on tripods and one on the wrist. Exterior cameras were first calibrated with a checkerboard using an open-source calibration routine; later the team added an automatic calibration with quality metrics. Tripods move, so the dataset recorded and re-estimated where they were.

Neither route removes the question. Randomizing buys tolerance at the cost of some accuracy; measuring buys accuracy only for as long as nobody touches the tripod.

Self-studyWhere the tangent comes from3 min▶

Picture the right-angled triangle in the figure: the camera at one corner, the true point straight ahead at distance d, and the apparent point off to the side. The angle at the camera is the yaw error. The side opposite it is the error at the table, and opposite over adjacent is the tangent, so the error is d x tan(angle). For small angles tan is almost the angle in radians, which is why the error grows almost exactly in proportion to both distance and angle. 1 degree is about 0.0175 rad, and 600 mm x 0.0175 is about 10.5 mm.

Part 3 · covers calibration as data, drift checks and wrist cameras

Calibration is data, not setup

5 min live

A calibration is true from the moment it is measured until the moment something moves. Every episode recorded in that window is consistent with it; every episode after an unrecorded move is not, and looks identical in the files. So the calibration id belongs in each episode's identity bundle (practitioner session 1), and the team needs a way to notice when the window closed.

LiveThe break button, read as a data problem3 min▶

On the sim-to-real bench in practitioner session 10, Wrenfield's best policy succeeds on 49 of 50 constructed real trials. One button moves the real camera 2 cm after calibration and changes nothing the policy reads about it. The result is 0 of 50. Nothing in the model changed; the transform it relied on stopped being true. A 2 cm shift against a 1.0 cm tolerance is a miss on every parcel.

Kress-Gazit et al. (2024) report the real-world version of the same lesson: re-running one policy on the same initial conditions after a lab reorganization dropped its success from 13 of 20 to 0 of 8, a distribution shift. The lab did not intend to change the experiment. The setting changed under it.

LiveWrist cameras: an extrinsic that changes every step2 min▶

A camera bolted to the gripper moves with it, so its position relative to the base is different at every timestep. You cannot store one transform for the episode. You compute it per step from the arm's own kinematics, the chain of joint angles from base to gripper, plus a fixed transform from gripper to camera that is calibrated once. That makes the wrist camera's extrinsic depend on the joint readings: a lost step in the joint lane (practitioner session 1) is also a lost camera pose. DROID's setup records both kinds, two exterior cameras on tripods and one on the wrist.

Self-studyA re-check schedule and a drift check4 min▶
  • Re-check on events, not only on a calendar. After any move of a tripod, a lens change, a new gripper, or a bench reorganization. Log the event even when nobody thinks it mattered.
  • A drift check with a fixed fiducial. Put a printed marker somewhere the arm never touches. At the start of each session, detect it and compare its position in the base frame with the position at calibration time. If it moved by more than a set fraction of the grasp tolerance, stop collecting and recalibrate.
  • Mark, do not delete. Episodes between the last good check and the failed one are suspect. Quarantine them with the reason (practitioner session 1); re-score them if the new calibration can be applied, drop them from training only with a record.
  • Store the quality metric. DROID's later automatic calibration came with quality metrics. A calibration id without a number for how good it was is half a record.

How often to re-check is a judgement per bench; there is no single published interval to copy. The fiducial check is cheap enough to run every session.

Build-along

Camera to base in numpy, then one degree of error

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Python 3.10 or later with numpy. You write the camera-to-base transform for Wrenfield's constructed bench as a 2D rotation plus a translation, move the parcel's address from one frame to the other, then turn the camera one degree further than the calibration says and measure how far the parcel appears to move at 60 cm.

Write a 2D rotation

Seen from above, a frame turns about one axis, so a rotation is a 2 x 2 matrix built from one angle. Write it as a function of degrees.

frames.py, part 1import numpy as np def rot2d(deg): a = np.radians(deg) return np.array([[np.cos(a), -np.sin(a)], [np.sin(a), np.cos(a)]])

Write the camera-to-base transform

Wrenfield's constructed calibration cal-1118: the camera faces back toward the arm (yaw 180 degrees) and sits at (0.90, 0.20) m in the base frame. Rotate, then shift.

frames.py, part 2# Constructed Wrenfield calibration cal-1118, metres, top-down view. cam_yaw_deg = 180.0 # camera faces back toward the robot cam_in_base = np.array([0.90, 0.20]) # camera position in the base frame def cam_to_base(p_cam, yaw_deg=cam_yaw_deg, t=cam_in_base): return rot2d(yaw_deg) @ p_cam + t parcel_cam = np.array([0.60, 0.00]) # 60 cm straight ahead of the camera parcel_base = cam_to_base(parcel_cam) print("parcel in camera frame (m):", parcel_cam) print("parcel in base frame (m): ", parcel_base.round(4))

Add one degree of yaw error

Pretend the camera is really one degree further round than the file says. Transform the same camera reading with the wrong angle and measure the distance between the two answers. Then check it against the tangent formula from Part 2.

frames.py, part 3# The camera is really 1 degree further round than the calibration says. seen_base = cam_to_base(parcel_cam, yaw_deg=cam_yaw_deg + 1.0) err_mm = np.linalg.norm(seen_base - parcel_base) * 1000 print("error from 1 degree of yaw (mm):", round(err_mm, 2)) print("600 * tan(1 deg) (mm): ", round(600 * np.tan(np.radians(1.0)), 2)) print("over the 10 mm tolerance:", err_mm > 10.0)

Run it and read the output

Run python3 frames.py from a scratch folder. The output below is what the script printed when this page was written. The rotation and the tangent agree to two decimals, and both say one degree is more than Wrenfield can forgive.

Expected outputparcel in camera frame (m): [0.6 0. ] parcel in base frame (m): [0.3 0.2] error from 1 degree of yaw (mm): 10.47 600 * tan(1 deg) (mm): 10.47 over the 10 mm tolerance: True

Extend it: the error across distances

Move the parcel to 30, 60 and 100 cm in front of the camera and print the error for the same one degree. Then find the largest yaw error your bench can afford at each distance. Run it and read the output; compare with the calculator in Part 2.

frames.py, part 4for d in (0.30, 0.60, 1.00): p = np.array([d, 0.0]) e = np.linalg.norm(cam_to_base(p, yaw_deg=181.0) - cam_to_base(p)) * 1000 max_yaw = np.degrees(np.arctan(0.010 / d)) print(f"{d:.2f} m: error {e:.2f} mm, max yaw {max_yaw:.2f} deg")

The habit to keep. Every position you store says its frame, and every episode says the calibration that links the frames. The 10.47 is a reason to write both down: under a degree of camera error is enough to spend the whole grasp tolerance.

Check yourself

Three questions

A Wrenfield policy outputs "target (0.30, 0.20) m" and the arm's controller accepts targets measured from the robot base. Which frame must that action be in?

The same parcel is (0.60, 0.00) m from the camera and (0.30, 0.20) m from the base. Units match in both; the meaning does not. The action column has to be in the frame the controller accepts, and say so.

The camera sits 60 cm from the work area and its yaw is off by 1 degree. Wrenfield's grasp tolerance is 1.0 cm (constructed). What happens?

The error is distance times the tangent of the angle. At 60 cm one degree is 10.47 mm, just over a 1.0 cm tolerance. Small angles are not small at the table.

Why does a wrist camera's extrinsic change every timestep?

The part that is fixed, gripper to camera, is calibrated once. The part that moves comes from the arm's own kinematics at each step, so a lost joint reading is also a lost camera pose.

The robot is a data loop

A warehouse row records something that already happened. A robot's data is different: the model's output moves an arm, the arm changes the world, and the next frame records what the model did. Every grasp is both a prediction and an experiment. Wrenfield Lab, a constructed robotics team teaching a two-arm robot to pick and pack parcels, is the case for all sixteen sessions; every Wrenfield number is generated in the page, not observed. Tonight is the loop, where the data team sits in it, and why a simulator that scores 100 percent is not the judge. Nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Wrenfield Lab, on the morning the first episodes land

Wrenfield Lab is constructed: a small team, one two-arm robot at a packing bench, a simulator on a workstation, and a data analyst who came from a retail warehouse. Her first ticket says "tell us whether the new grasp policy is ready". What arrives is not a fact table. It is a few hundred simulated episodes, twenty recorded demonstrations, a calibration file, and a spreadsheet of fifty real attempts with a tick or a cross. The six leader sessions are about reading that pile as a decision-maker; the ten practitioner sessions are about building on it.

LiveWhy the warehouse instinct misfires here4 min▶

A warehouse analyst trusts three things without naming them. A row records an event that would have happened whether or not anyone measured it. A row has a stable identity: an order id, a customer. And the metric is cheap to recompute on fresh data every week.

Robot data breaks all three. The model's own output changed what was recorded next, so the data depends on which policy was running. The identity of an episode is not one key but a bundle: which robot, which calibration, which version of the policy, which operator, which lighting. And the metric that matters, "did it work on the real robot", costs minutes of a person resetting the table per trial, so most teams have tens of trials, not millions of rows.

Real world

Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023), the paper that introduced a low-cost two-arm teleoperation rig and the ACT policy, evaluated each real task with 25 trials. Their 50 demonstrations per task were 10 to 20 minutes of data but took 30 to 60 minutes of wall-clock time because of resets and operator mistakes. That is the economics of physical data: the expensive part is the world, not the storage.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Episode. One attempt at a task from start to finish: every camera frame, every joint reading and every command, on one clock, plus whether it succeeded. The unit of robot data (practitioner session 1).
  • Demonstration. An episode in which a person drove the robot, usually through teleoperation. Demonstrations are the labelled training data of imitation learning (leader session 2).
  • Policy. The model that maps what the robot senses to what it does next. In Wrenfield's bench it is deliberately tiny so you can watch it learn.
  • Rollout. The policy running on its own, in simulation or on the real robot. An evaluation is a set of rollouts with a success rule.
  • Embodiment. The particular body: arm, gripper, cameras and where they are mounted. Data from one embodiment does not drop straight into another (practitioner session 4).
  • Sim-to-real gap. The difference between how a policy does in the simulator and on the real robot. The p10 bench prints it on every rung.
  • Constructed. Wrenfield Lab and every number about it are invented so that the truth is knowable. The research numbers are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers the loop and the data team's seats

Five turns of the loop, each handing the next a guess

15 min live

The clearest way to see a robot as a data problem is to draw the loop it runs. Cameras and joint sensors sense. A policy decides where to move. The arm acts, and the world changes. Everything is logged as an episode. The team learns from the episodes and ships a new policy, which collects different data next time. Each turn is right most of the time and wrong in ways the next turn inherits. Under every turn sits a piece of data work.

LiveWhat each turn hands on, and how it goes wrong5 min▶

Sense. Cameras see the parcel in pixels; joint encoders report where the arm thinks it is. Neither tells you where the parcel is in the robot's own coordinates until a calibration connects them. Practitioner session 2 is that connection.

Decide and act. The policy turns observations into the next target for the arm, many times a second. Zhao and colleagues recorded and controlled their rig at 50 Hz, so an 8 to 14 second task is 400 to 700 decisions. A small error early compounds, because each decision starts from where the last one left the arm.

Log and learn. The episode is the record of all of that on one clock, plus a judgement: did it work? The next policy is trained from those episodes. Change the policy and you change what gets logged, so a dataset is always a dataset of some policy, never of the world in general.

LiveThe five seats, as five questions4 min▶
  • Calibration: when was each camera last calibrated, and would anyone notice if one moved? Wrenfield's break button (p10) moves one by 2 cm and success falls from 49 of 50 to 0.
  • Demonstrations: who drove the robot, how many episodes were thrown away, and does the set cover the conditions the robot will meet?
  • Episodes: can you replay any attempt from every camera on one clock, and does each one say which robot, calibration and policy produced it?
  • Evaluation trials: how many, under which conditions, and who defined success? A rate without a trial count is not a result.
  • Simulation: what was randomized, how wide, and how was the result checked on the real robot?
Self-studyAn episode is not a row4 min▶

In the two-arm rig of Zhao et al. (2023), an observation is the follower arms' joint positions plus images from four cameras, and the action recorded is the leader arms' joint positions, the ones the person moved. The authors explain why it must be the leader's: the force the robot applies is implicit in the difference between leader and follower.

So the smallest unit the data team works with is a time-indexed bundle of images, joint vectors and commands, with a success flag at the end that a person decided. Warehouse habits about one row per event and a primary key have to be rebuilt around time and around the robot that produced it.

Part 2 · covers the sim-to-real gap, from research and from the bench

The simulator is a training ground, never the judge

10 min live

Simulation makes episodes cheap: no resets, no broken parcels, thousands an hour. That is why almost every robotics team uses it. But a policy can be perfect in the simulator and mediocre on the real table, because the simulator's lighting, camera position and lens are not the real ones. The research answer is domain randomization: vary the simulator so widely that the real world looks like one more variation. Wrenfield's bench shows each step of that, with the real-table result next to the simulated one.

LiveWhat the research found, read at the source4 min▶

Tobin and colleagues (2017) trained an object detector on simulated images only, with deliberately unrealistic random textures, random lights, random distractor objects and a camera placed at random inside a 10 by 5 by 10 centimetre box around its rough real position. On real images it located objects to within 1.5 cm on average, and a robot using it picked the target in 38 of 40 cluttered trials.

The same paper is honest about the gap. In simulation the detector's error was 0.3 to 0.5 cm, so it still over-fitted the simulator. And since no real data was used in training, the authors note that no validation on real data could be done during training. The real check came afterwards, on a real robot, in tens of trials.

LiveThree misreadings to stop on sight3 min▶

"It works 100 percent in simulation." The simulator measured how well the policy fits the simulator. Ask for the real number and the trial count next to it.

"More randomization is always safer." Mehta and colleagues (2019) report that randomizing uniformly can give "suboptimal, high-variance policies". Wrenfield's over-randomized run scores 47.9 percent in simulation and 19 of 50 on the real table. How wide is wide enough is an open question (practitioner session 6).

"We will fix the real gap with more simulation." Some gaps only real data can reveal. Wrenfield's real lens magnifies by 8 percent and no simulator setting knew it; twenty real demonstrations fixed what no width could (practitioner session 10).

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

When general-purpose robots will work outside controlled cells, and whether scale of data alone will get there, are open questions. Forecasts have been made and missed in both directions, and the people closest to the work disagree. No page here gives a date.

What the published evidence does show is narrower. Pooling data from many robots helped in one large study and hurt in another part of the same study: the Open X-Embodiment collaboration (2023) reports a 50 percent higher success rate on small-data robots and a model that underfit and did worse on the large-data ones (practitioner session 4). A leader should expect "it depends on the robot and the data" to be the honest answer for some time.

Part 3 · build-along

Five questions to put to any robot data claim

★ 17 min · everyone builds

No code. Take one claim you have seen or will be asked to sign off, about a robot, a policy or a dataset, and run it through five questions in order. The figure is why the order matters: one sentence like "it picks parcels 90 percent of the time" hides five decisions somebody made.

Write the claim down in one sentence

Exactly as it was made: "the robot picks parcels 90 percent of the time", "the new policy is twice as good", "trained on a million episodes". Underline the number and the comparison word.

Ask how many trials

Write the count next to the rate. 9 of 10 and 90 of 100 are both 90 percent and are very different evidence; leader session 3 shows how different. If there is no count, write "no count" and stop there.

Ask which conditions

Lighting, parcel sizes, where the parcels sat, which robot, which day. A rate from a tidy demo cell says little about a cluttered packing bench at night.

Ask who defined success, and who judged it

Was a parcel dropped after lifting a success? Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) recommend a clear, detailed, unambiguous definition, written by the person who designed the behaviour but judged by someone else.

Ask about human help, and sim or real

Was anyone teleoperating, resetting or nudging during the attempts? Was the number measured in a simulator? Leader session 4 is about the videos where these answers are hidden.

Commit to a guess about Wrenfield

Wrenfield's lighting-randomized policy got 37 of 50 real grasps. Write down whether you think one more change (randomizing the camera position too) will move that a lot or a little. Practitioner session 6 checks the guess.

The habit to keep. When someone shows you a robot number, do not ask whether it is good. Ask how many trials, which conditions, whose definition of success, what human help, and sim or real. A rate with no trial count is not a small claim; it is no claim.

Check yourself

Three questions

Wrenfield's first policy (constructed) grasps 100 percent of simulated parcels and 29 of 50 real ones. What is the right reading?

The simulator had one lighting level and one camera position, so the policy never learned to cope with others. Fifty trials do say something: 29 of 50 has a 95 percent interval of about 44 to 71 percent, far from 100 (leader session 3).

Why must a robot dataset record which policy was running when each episode was collected?

The loop feeds on itself. A warehouse row records an event that would have happened anyway; a robot episode records what one policy did and what followed from it.

Tobin et al. (2017) report a detector trained only in simulation that located real objects to within 1.5 cm and supported 38 grasps in 40 trials. What did the paper also say?

The authors are explicit about the remaining gap. Their ablation found camera randomization gave "a slight accuracy boost" (2.0 cm error without it against 1.3 cm with it, objects alone), not a loss.

Before session 2

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one claim and on the loop.

Run the five questions on one real claim

Pick a public statement or video about a robot learning a task, from any source, and write one line per question. Do not judge the claim; mark which questions it leaves unanswered.

Draw your own loop

For any process you know that learns from its own outputs (a recommender, a pricing rule), draw the five turns and mark where its data depends on its last decision. Leader session 5 comes back to this.

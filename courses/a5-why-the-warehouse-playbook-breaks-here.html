Why the warehouse playbook breaks here

A warehouse data project leans on five things nobody writes down: where the row came from, what makes two rows the same, who says a label is true, whether the report changes the data, and what a mistake costs. Robot data changes all five. Tonight takes them one mechanism at a time, each drawn twice: once as it works in the warehouse, once as it works at a robot cell. Wrenfield Lab, the constructed robotics team from the earlier sessions, supplies the worked examples; every Wrenfield number is generated, not observed. Safety standards are described as reported; nothing here is legal or investment advice.

Part 0

Wrenfield's analyst brings her old checklist

Wrenfield Lab is constructed. Its data analyst came from a retail warehouse, where she ran a clean playbook: land the source tables, key every row, trust the labels the systems wrote, refresh the dashboard weekly, fix mistakes in next week's report. Six weeks into robotics she has a list of things that went wrong, and every one traces back to a step of that playbook that quietly assumed something robot data does not give you. The five mechanisms below are her list, cleaned up.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Five mechanisms where a conventional data project changes shape at a robot: the source is sensors on one clock steered by the policy, not events people meant; the identity of an episode is a bundle of robot, calibration, policy, operator, scene, success rule and software, not one key; the ground truth is a flag a person set by a versioned rule; the feedback loop means data from a new policy is a different sample; and a wrong action moves an arm near people, so evaluation has to count where they were. A worksheet to run any warehouse project through all five, and the one question to ask a robotics team on day one.

LiveWhy "same skills, new domain" is half right3 min▶

Most of what a good warehouse data person knows carries over: lineage, versioning, joins that fail loudly, metrics with written definitions. What does not carry over is the set of defaults underneath. In a warehouse they are true often enough that nobody checks them. At a robot cell they are false often enough that checking them is the job.

The honest framing for a leader is not "robotics data is harder". It is "five defaults flip, and each one needs a specific replacement". Leader session 6 turns that into a transfer map; tonight is the mechanism behind each flip.

Real world

The Open X-Embodiment Collaboration (2023) pooled more than a million real robot trajectories: 60 datasets from 22 robot types across 21 institutions. To train one model on them, the authors aligned the data only coarsely, one canonical camera view per dataset and a shared 7-number action, and they state the limits plainly: "we do not align the coordinate frames across datasets", and camera observations "still vary substantially". A warehouse merge of 60 sources would never ship with that caveat; in robotics it is the honest state of the art.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Episode. One attempt at a task, start to finish, every sensor on one clock, plus a success flag (practitioner session 1).
  • Policy. The model that maps what the robot senses to what it does next. It also decides, without meaning to, which situations end up in the data.
  • Calibration. The file that says where each camera really is relative to the robot. Move a camera without updating it and every frame lies quietly (practitioner session 2).
  • Success rule. The written definition a person applies to mark an episode a success or a failure. It has a version, like code.
  • Intervention. A person taking over or stopping the robot during a rollout. A useful label, and a biased one (practitioner session 8).
  • Protective stop. The robot halting itself because a safety function tripped. A logged event with a cost (practitioner session 9).
  • Constructed. Wrenfield Lab and every number about it are invented so the truth is knowable. Research numbers are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers the source and the identity

Who wrote the row, and what makes two rows the same

10 min live

The first two mechanisms are the ones a data engineer meets in week one. A warehouse row is an event someone meant, a customer buying, a picker scanning, and it would have happened whether or not anyone logged it. A robot frame is a reading from a sensor on a shared clock, taken a moment after the policy moved the arm, so the data already depends on the policy that collected it (leader session 1). And the identity of that frame is not a customer id: it is everything that would make the same frame mean something different.

LiveThe source: sensors, a clock, and a policy in the loop4 min▶

A warehouse source is a system of record: something happened, a system wrote it down. The right questions are about completeness and latency. A robot source is a set of sensors sampled on one clock: cameras, joint encoders, the commands sent to the arm. The right questions are about alignment (are the frames from the same instant?) and about authorship: which policy was steering when these frames were taken?

That second question has no warehouse equivalent. If policy v3 tends to approach parcels from the left, the dataset holds many left approaches and few right ones, not because the world is like that but because v3 is. Leader session 1 called this the loop feeding on itself. For the data team it means a dataset always carries a policy tag, and a sample statistic is a statistic of that policy's behaviour.

Clocks, sampling rates and resampling sensor streams are their own discipline; learn IoT data owns them.

LiveThe identity: a bundle, not a key4 min▶

Practitioner session 1 lists what an episode needs beside its frames: the robot, the calibration, the policy or the operator who drove it, the scene, the success rule and the software version. In a warehouse those would be "metadata", nice to have. Here they are part of the identity, because changing any one of them changes what the frames mean.

Wrenfield's bench makes the point brutally. Its best policy (constructed) grasps 49 of 50 real parcels. Move one camera 2 cm and do not record it, and the same policy grasps 0 of 50. Nothing in the episode table changed: same robot id, same policy version, same columns. Only the calibration, which was not in the key, was wrong.

The replacement for "primary key" is a composite identity that every episode carries, and a rule that two episodes are comparable only when the parts you care about match.

Self-studyWhen the scene is part of the identity3 min▶

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) give an example that would never happen to a warehouse table. A policy evaluated at 13 of 20 was re-run on the same initial conditions after the lab was reorganized, and scored 0 of 8. Same policy, same robot, same task; the room had changed around it.

The lesson for identity is that "scene" is not a free-text note. It is the thing that lets you say whether two numbers were measured in the same world. The authors recommend matching initial conditions with image overlays or markings, which is the robot version of pinning a snapshot date on a report.

Part 2 · covers the ground truth and the feedback loop

Who says it worked, and what the policy chose to show you

9 min live

The next two mechanisms are where warehouse statistics quietly stop applying. A warehouse label is usually a fact a system recorded; a robot success flag is a decision a person made by a rule. And a warehouse report does not change which orders arrive next week, while a new robot policy changes which situations get recorded at all.

LiveGround truth is a person applying a rule3 min▶

In DROID, Khazatsky and colleagues (2024) built a large in-the-wild dataset: about 76,000 demonstration trajectories, roughly 350 hours, 564 scenes, 50 data collectors over 12 months. Each collector marked each episode a success or a failure. About 16,000 unsuccessful trajectories were released separately, and failed episodes were excluded from policy training. That is a sound protocol, and it means the training set is filtered by 50 people's reading of "success".

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) treat the definition as an engineering artefact: clear, detailed and unambiguous, written by whoever designed the behaviour, applied by someone who did not. For a data team the practical consequence is simple. The success flag needs a rule version beside it, exactly as a metric needs a definition version, because changing the rule relabels history.

LiveThe feedback loop: each policy is a new sample3 min▶

A warehouse forecast trained on last year predicts next year reasonably because the forecast does not choose next year's customers. A robot policy does choose its own situations: where the arm goes, which grasps it attempts, how often it ends up near the edge of the bench. Ship policy v2 and the episodes it collects describe v2's world, which may include places v1 never went.

Two practical consequences. First, an evaluation of v1 says little about the situations v2 will create; the comparison must be run under matched initial conditions (leader session 3). Second, pooling episodes across policy versions mixes samples, so every training set needs the policy mix written down, like a sampling frame.

Interventions make the loop subtler. A person takes over only where a person noticed something; the intervention log is a valuable label and a biased one, and practitioner session 8 treats it that way. Learn autonomous driving covers the fleet-scale version, disengagements.

Self-studyWhere the label filter and the loop meet3 min▶

Put DROID's protocol and the feedback loop together and a subtle effect appears. Training only on successes is a sound default, and it also means the model never sees the situations where people failed. If the next policy then visits more of those situations, the data that would have taught it is exactly the data that was set aside.

This is not a criticism of the dataset, which released its failures separately so others can study them. It is a reason for a data team to keep failures, label them by rule version, and decide on purpose what is excluded, rather than let the filter be an accident of collection.

Part 3 · covers the cost of being wrong

A wrong row is corrected next week; a wrong action moves an arm

6 min live

The last mechanism changes what "good enough" means. A wrong number in a warehouse report costs a correction note. A wrong action at a robot cell moves metal, sometimes in a space a person shares. That asymmetry belongs in the evaluation itself: where people were during the trials, how often the robot stopped itself, and which errors happened close to someone.

LiveWhat the standards describe, as reported3 min▶

Robots that share space with people are governed by standards, and a data leader should know their shape, not their numbers. As reported (the documents are paywalled and were not read here): ISO/TS 15066:2016 describes power and force limiting as one of four collaborative operation modes, with maximum contact force and pressure tabulated per body region, set from pain-onset thresholds and described as preliminary. ISO 10218-1 and -2 were revised in 2025, and that collaborative content is reported to be consolidated into Part 1. Re-check the current catalogue before relying on any of this.

Nothing on this page is legal advice or a compliance checklist. The point for a data team is what those mechanisms produce: stops, slow-downs and force-limit trips, each one a timestamped event in a log someone should be reading.

Real world

Behrens and colleagues (2022), reported from the abstract, ran a study with 112 subjects toward replacing those preliminary values, and found that pain thresholds differ between men and women in some body regions. Even the limits themselves are still being measured; a safety threshold is evidence with a sample behind it, like everything else here.

LiveThe data consequence: asymmetric cost changes evaluation3 min▶

Errors are not equal. A failed grasp at the far side of the bench costs a reset. The same failure while a person reaches into the tote costs something else entirely. A single success rate averages those together, which is why evaluation has to record where people were during each trial (practitioner session 9).

Stops are data, not noise. Protective stops are logged events. A stop rate needs a denominator, per hour of shared operation or per pick, or it cannot be compared across weeks or cells.

The fix is not next week's report. In a warehouse you can ship, watch and correct. Near people, the evidence has to come before the change, which pushes more weight onto the small, careful evaluations of leader session 3.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

How much of the warehouse playbook needs rewriting is itself contested. One view holds that pooling ever-larger robot datasets will wash out per-robot quirks, the way large text corpora washed out per-source quirks. Open X-Embodiment (2023) supports half of that: pooled training helped on small-data robots (a 50 percent higher mean success) and underfit on the large-data ones at the model size tested.

The other view holds that identity, calibration and success rules will always need per-cell care, because the world does not normalize. Both views are held by serious people. A leader's safe position is to fund the bookkeeping either way: it is cheap, and every later decision depends on it.

Build-along

Run one warehouse project through the five mechanisms

★ 17 min · everyone builds

No code. Pick one data project you have run or overseen in a warehouse, retail or operations setting: a demand forecast, a pick-rate dashboard, a returns classifier. Imagine handing its playbook to a robotics team. For each of the five mechanisms, write the assumption your project made, what breaks at a robot cell, and what replaces it. The figure shows the worksheet with one row filled in for Wrenfield.

Name the project and its decision

One line: what the project measured and what decision it fed. "Weekly pick-rate per picker, used to plan shifts." Keep it real and specific; a vague project produces a vague worksheet.

Source and identity

Write who or what produced each row, and what made two rows the same. Then ask the robot version: which policy was steering when the data was taken, and which of robot, calibration, policy, operator, scene, success rule and software would have to match for two records to be comparable?

Ground truth

Write where your labels came from. If a system recorded them, write "fact". If a person decided, write the rule they used and whether it was versioned. At a robot cell, assume a person decided, and write the rule version you would require.

Feedback loop

Did your project's output change the data it would see next? A pick-rate dashboard usually does not; a slotting optimizer that moves stock does. At a robot cell, assume it always does, and write how you would label which policy collected each episode.

Cost of being wrong

Write what a wrong number cost in your project and how fast it was corrected. Then write what the robot version would need recorded during evaluation: where people were, stops per hour of shared operation, errors near someone.

Write your day-one question

Look at the five rows and pick the one where your project's assumption would fail most quietly. Turn it into one question to ask a robotics team on day one. Wrenfield's analyst would ask: "for any episode, can you name the robot, calibration, policy and success rule behind it?"

The habit to keep. When a robotics team hands you a dataset, do not start with the schema. Start with five questions: who steered while it was recorded, what makes two episodes the same, which rule set each success flag, which policy versions are mixed in, and where people were when it went wrong.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why is a robot dataset a sample of the policy that collected it, rather than a sample of the world?

The policy is in the loop. A warehouse row records an event that would have happened anyway; a robot episode records what one policy did and what followed, so every dataset needs its policy mix written down.

Why does a robot success flag need a versioned rule beside it?

In DROID (Khazatsky et al. 2024) collectors marked each episode success or failure, and failures were kept out of training. Kress-Gazit et al. (2024) ask for a clear, detailed, unambiguous definition applied by someone other than its author. Change the rule and history changes.

How does the cost of being wrong change how a robot policy is evaluated?

A wrong row is corrected next week; a wrong action moves an arm. Protective stops are logged events, and a stop rate needs a denominator such as hours of shared operation. Per-region limits are in paywalled standards, reported here, and are not quoted on this page.

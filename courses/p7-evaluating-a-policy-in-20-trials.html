LiveWhat evaluation reports usually leave out3 min▶

A success rate is the number everyone quotes and the number that, on its own, says the least. It needs its trial count, the conditions the trials started from, the rule that decided success, and some statistical analysis. Without those four, two rates from two teams are not comparable, and two rates from one team may not be either.

Real world

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024), in "Robot Learning as an Empirical Science", observe that robot learning papers usually report a success rate with little information on the number of runs, the initial conditions, the success criteria or any statistical analysis. They recommend reporting all four, plus complementary metrics and qualitative failure modes. Their practices for running the trials themselves (blind, interleaved, matched, one session) are the design half of tonight.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Trial. One rollout of a policy from a recorded initial condition, judged once by a written rule.
  • Interval. The range of true success rates that could plausibly have produced the count you saw. Here, 95 percent intervals.
  • Wald interval. Rate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors. Fine at large n and middling rates; broken at the ends.
  • Wilson interval. The score interval of Wilson (1927, reported). Keeps a width at 0 of n and n of n.
  • Fisher's exact test. For two independent counts: how often a split at least this uneven appears when the two policies are equally good.
  • Discordant pair. On one matched start, one policy succeeded and the other failed. A paired test reads only these.
Part 1 · covers the Wilson interval and where the Wald interval fails

At 20 of 20, the textbook interval says "exactly 100 percent"

8 min live

The interval most people were taught is the Wald interval: rate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, with the standard error computed from the observed rate. At 20 of 20 the observed rate is 1, the standard error is 0, and the interval is 1 to 1. A perfect score from twenty trials becomes a certainty. Small robot evaluations produce perfect scores and zero scores often, so the failure lands exactly where you work.

LiveThe Wilson interval, as a formula you can write down4 min▶

Wilson's trick is to invert the test instead of plugging in the observed rate. The interval is every true rate p for which your count k of n would not be surprising at the 95 percent level. Solving that quadratic gives a centre pulled towards one half and a half-width that never reaches zero.

Wilson 95% score intervalp_hat = k / n z = 1.959964 # two-sided 95% den = 1 + z^2 / n centre = (p_hat + z^2 / (2 n)) / den half = (z / den) * sqrt( p_hat (1 - p_hat) / n + z^2 / (4 n^2) ) lo, hi = max(0, centre - half), min(1, centre + half)

At k = 0 the term under the root is z^2 / (4 n^2), not zero, so the upper end is z^2 / (n + z^2). At n = 20 that is 0.161: the bound the engine checks against on every page load.

Self-studyWhy not the exact (Clopper-Pearson) interval?3 min▶

There are several honest intervals for a proportion. The "exact" interval built from the binomial distribution guarantees at least 95 percent coverage and is therefore wider than it needs to be on average. Wilson's aims at 95 percent on average and is a little narrower. Both keep width at the ends; the Wald interval is the one to retire. The engine and the bench use Wilson, and leader session 3 shows the same values; pick one method per team, write it in the evaluation plan, and never switch after seeing the data.

Part 2 · covers Fisher's exact test, paired designs and trials per arm

Comparing two policies: the test, the design, the price

9 min live

Two intervals that overlap are a warning, not a test. Fisher's exact test asks the question directly for two independent counts. But the stronger move is in the design: run both policies from the same starts, interleaved, and the comparison stops paying for the variance between starts. Then you can say what a difference costs in trials before the afternoon begins.

LiveReading Fisher's p at three splits3 min▶

Fisher's exact test fixes the total number of successes across both policies and asks: if the two were equally good, how likely is a split at least as uneven as this one? It is exact, so it stays honest at twenty trials where approximate chi-square tests do not.

  • 18 of 20 against 15 of 20: p = 0.4075. A split this uneven turns up about two times in five between equal policies. No ranking.
  • 18 of 20 against 5 of 20: p = 0.00007. Twenty trials can separate policies; they just need a large gap to do it.
  • 13 of 20 against 14 of 20: p = 1.0. Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) use this pair to say "we cannot state that one policy outperforms the other". Type it into the widget.

Leave the widget at its defaults and read the last line: at these same two rates, the first n per arm where the rounded expected counts clear 0.05 is 55. That is the size at which the expected result only just clears the line, so a real run of that size clears it roughly as often as not. Plan above it, or accept that the afternoon answers a smaller question.

LiveThe paired design: same starts, interleaved, blind, one session3 min▶

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) recommend four practices for comparing policies, and each removes one way the room can impersonate the policy:

  • Match the initial conditions. Both policies start from the same recorded parcel poses and scene, reproduced with image overlays or floor markings. Hard starts and easy starts are now shared, not split unevenly by chance.
  • Interleave the rollouts. A and B alternate through the session, so drift in light, battery or table wear lands on both.
  • Keep the evaluator blind. The person judging success does not know which policy ran, and the person who designed the behaviour writes the success rule but does not apply it.
  • Evaluate all policies in one session. A number from last month is a different world.

Matching also changes the analysis. Each start now produces a pair of outcomes, and a paired test such as McNemar's test (standard) uses only the discordant pairs: starts where one policy succeeded and the other failed. Starts both policies pass, or both fail, say nothing about which is better and drop out. Fisher's test treats the two arms as independent samples and cannot use that structure. With matched starts, report both, and say which one your plan named in advance.

Self-studyOne test, named before the afternoon3 min▶

With forty trials it is easy to find some cut of the data that crosses 0.05: dim light only, left-edge parcels only, the first ten trials only. Each extra look is another draw. The defence is boring and effective: write the primary comparison, the test and n per arm in the plan before the first trial, and label everything else as exploratory. Per-condition breakdowns are still valuable; they are where failure modes show up. They are not where a ranking comes from.

Part 3 · covers the bench at 20 and 200 trials, shift, and research trial counts

What the bench shows when the truth is known

3 min live

A real team never sees a policy's true rate. Wrenfield's bench (constructed, practitioner session 10) can, because it can run 2,000 constructed trials per rung. Score the same rungs at 20 trials and at 200, and you see what the interval has been warning about: at 20 the three best rungs look alike; at 200 they separate in the order the truth says.

LiveThe 20-trial row, taken apart3 min▶

Run PAI.ladder({nTrials: 20}) on the bench engine and rungs 2, 3 and 4 score 18, 19 and 20 of 20 (constructed, seed 1122). Their true rates are 0.716, 0.908 and 0.951. Put the three counts in the comparator above: every pair overlaps, and no pair clears 0.05. The evaluation did nothing wrong; it was simply too small to see a difference of 0.235 in true rate between rungs 2 and 4 (0.951 minus 0.716).

At 200 trials the same rungs score 147, 190 and 197 of 200, and the ordering matches the truth. And the size of the sample is not the only lever: at 50 trials with seed 7, rungs 1 and 2 tie at 34 each, although on the canon seed rung 2 is the better rung in truth (0.716 against 0.656). Change the seed and the tie can move. A small evaluation is one draw from a distribution of evaluations; the interval is the honest description of that distribution.

Live13 of 20, then 0 of 8: shift is not chance2 min▶

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) re-ran a policy on the same initial conditions after the lab was reorganized. Success went from 13 of 20 to 0 of 8. No interval width explains that; the world changed under the policy. This is why the plan records the scene, and why "one session" is a rule: an interval quantifies sampling noise inside one world and says nothing about a move to another. When a number collapses like this, the next question is not "how many more trials" but "what changed in the room".

Self-studyHow many trials published work actually runs3 min▶

Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023) evaluated each real task of their two-arm system with 25 trials (one seed). Khazatsky and colleagues (2024), the DROID dataset team, ran 10 rollouts per task setting per method in their A/B policy comparisons. Both are reasonable for the cost of real trials, and both are sizes where a perfect 25 of 25 only bounds the true rate above 0.867, and a gap between two methods within one setting has to be very large to clear a test. Read such results as evidence summed across many settings and tasks, which is how those authors present them, not as a ranking from any one cell.

Self-studyThe Bayesian alternative: teach both4 min▶

Kress-Gazit and colleagues (2024) work their examples with Bayesian analysis, starting from a uniform prior over the success rate, and mention frequentist intervals as the alternative. With a uniform prior, k successes in n trials give a Beta(k + 1, n - k + 1) posterior for the rate. You can then ask the question a manager actually asks, "how likely is it that A's true rate exceeds B's?", by sampling both posteriors and counting.

The two schools answer slightly different questions. Wilson and Fisher say how surprising the data would be under a hypothesis; the posterior says how plausible each rate is given the data and the prior. At twenty trials they agree on the headline: a lot of uncertainty remains. Careful people use both, and the field has not settled on one. What matters is that the plan names the method before the trials run, and that the report shows the counts so a reader can redo it the other way.

Build-along

Wilson and Fisher in plain Python, then Wrenfield's evaluation plan

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Python 3.10 or later, standard library only: math for the statistics and random for the order. If scipy is installed you can cross-check Fisher against it, but nothing here needs it. You reproduce 0.699 to 0.972 and p = 0.4075, then write the plan for Wrenfield's afternoon. The figure is the schedule the plan produces: ten matched starts, both policies on each, the order shuffled per pair, the evaluator blind.

Write the Wilson interval

Make a scratch folder and create evalplan.py. The function is the formula from Part 1, line for line. Clamp to 0 and 1 so rounding never prints a negative lower bound.

evalplan.py, part 1import math import random Z = 1.959963984540054 # two-sided 95% def wilson(k, n, z=Z): p = k / n den = 1 + z * z / n centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / den half = (z / den) * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) return max(0.0, centre - half), min(1.0, centre + half)

Write Fisher's exact test, two-sided

Fix the row and column totals, compute the probability of every possible split with math.comb, and add up the splits no more likely than the one observed. The small tolerance stops floating-point noise from dropping a table that ties the observed one.

evalplan.py, part 2def fisher_two_sided(a, n1, b, n2): K, N = a + b, n1 + n2 def prob(x): return math.comb(n1, x) * math.comb(n2, K - x) / math.comb(N, K) observed = prob(a) lo, hi = max(0, K - n2), min(K, n1) return min(1.0, sum(prob(x) for x in range(lo, hi + 1) if prob(x) <= observed * (1 + 1e-7)))

Reproduce the numbers and read the output

Print Wrenfield's two intervals and both Fisher comparisons, then run python3 evalplan.py. The output below was produced by running exactly this file with Python 3.11 when this page was written.

evalplan.py, part 3lo, hi = wilson(18, 20) print(f"Wilson 18/20: {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f}") lo, hi = wilson(15, 20) print(f"Wilson 15/20: {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f}") print(f"Fisher 18/20 vs 15/20: p = {fisher_two_sided(18, 20, 15, 20):.4f}") print(f"Fisher 13/20 vs 14/20: p = {fisher_two_sided(13, 20, 14, 20):.4f}")

Write the evaluation plan as code

Create plan.py. The plan is data before it is a document: ten initial conditions crossing parcel position, rotation, distractors and light; one success rule with a time limit and "no human touch"; two passes so each arm gets 20 trials; a fixed seed so the shuffled order can be reproduced and audited; and blind codes for the evaluator. The conditions and codes are Wrenfield's constructed choices.

plan.pyimport random rng = random.Random(7) # constructed Wrenfield plan; fixed seed so the order is reproducible conditions = [ ("C01", "parcel centre, normal light"), ("C02", "parcel centre, dim light"), ("C03", "parcel left edge, normal light"), ("C04", "parcel left edge, dim light"), ("C05", "parcel right edge, normal light"), ("C06", "parcel right edge, dim light"), ("C07", "parcel rotated 45 degrees, normal"), ("C08", "parcel rotated 45 degrees, dim"), ("C09", "two distractor parcels, normal"), ("C10", "two distractor parcels, dim"), ] success_rule = "parcel in tote, tote upright, within 30 s, no human touch" reps = 2 # each condition twice per policy: 20 trials per arm codes = {"A": "P-K", "B": "P-Q"} # the evaluator sees only these codes schedule = [] for rep in range(reps): for cid, _ in conditions: pair = ["A", "B"] rng.shuffle(pair) # which policy goes first is random per pair schedule += [(cid, p) for p in pair] print("success rule:", success_rule) print("trials per arm:", sum(1 for _, p in schedule if p == "A")) for i, (cid, p) in enumerate(schedule[:6], start=1): print(f"{i:02d} {cid} {codes[p]}")
Expected outputsuccess rule: parcel in tote, tote upright, within 30 s, no human touch trials per arm: 20 01 C01 P-K 02 C01 P-Q 03 C02 P-Q 04 C02 P-K 05 C03 P-K 06 C03 P-Q

Extend it: decide n per arm honestly

The plan above gives 20 per arm because that is what one afternoon buys. Put Wrenfield's expected rates into the comparator in Part 2 and write the result into the plan as a sentence: "At 20 per arm this evaluation can detect only a large gap; a 15-point gap would need more than twice the trials per arm." Then add a column for the paired outcome per start, so the discordant pairs can be counted after the session. Run it and read your output: your conditions and order will differ if you change the seed.

The habit to keep. Write the plan, the test and n per arm before the first trial, and keep the seed. An evaluation whose order, rule and analysis can be replayed by someone else is worth more than one with ten extra trials and no record.

Check yourself

Three questions

A policy scores 20 of 20. What does the Wald interval report, and what is wrong with it?

The Wald interval computes its width from the observed rate, which is 1. Wilson keeps a width set by n: 0.839 to 1 at 20 of 20.

Wrenfield's new policy A scores 18 of 20 and the current policy B scores 15 of 20. What should the report say?

A split this uneven appears about two times in five between equally good policies. The honest report gives both intervals, the test, and the cost of a decisive answer.

Why run both policies from the same initial conditions, interleaved, in one session?

Matching removes condition variance from the comparison. Fisher's test treats the arms as independent and does not need matching; a paired test such as McNemar's uses it.

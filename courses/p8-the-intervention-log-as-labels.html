The intervention log as labels

When a policy runs and a person watching takes over just before it fails, the log records far more than an embarrassment. It records where the policy was about to go wrong and what should have happened instead: two labels, collected exactly where the policy is weakest. Tonight you learn to read that log as training data, to see the operator's judgement hidden inside every intervention count, and to keep the corrections and the evaluation on separate paths. Wrenfield Lab and every number in its log are constructed; no published intervention figures are quoted here.

Part 0

Wrenfield's operators keep a log

Wrenfield Lab (constructed) now runs its parcel-picking policy on the bench every day, with an operator beside the arm holding the teleoperation controls. When the gripper drifts toward the edge of the tote, the operator takes over, finishes the pick by hand, and hands control back. Each takeover writes a row. After a month the team has a few hundred rows, and two questions: can we train on them, and does a falling intervention count mean the policy got better?

★ What you walk out with tonight. A drawing of one takeover as two labels on one clock: a state label ("about to fail here") and an action label ("do this instead"). A live model that shows how the same policy produces very different intervention rates under a cautious and a loose operator. The four fields every intervention row needs. And a pandas script that computes intervention rate and correction share per operator on a constructed log, then splits corrections for training away from hands-off trials for evaluation.

LiveWhy a takeover is not just a failure count3 min▶

Demonstrations collected up front show the robot what good looks like in the situations the demonstrator happened to visit. A policy running on its own drifts into situations nobody demonstrated, and small errors compound. An operator who takes over at that moment produces exactly the data the original set lacked: the hard state, and the recovery from it. People correcting a policy during its own rollouts and training on the corrections is a recognised family of methods in robot learning. Tonight is about the data side of it: what the log contains, what it silently leaves out, and how not to fool yourself with it.

Real world

In the teleoperation rig of Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (2023), the recorded action is the leader arm the person moves, not the follower arm that obeys it. The same idea makes a takeover usable: if the operator corrects through the same leader arm, the correction lands in the log in the same units as every demonstration. The authors also report 30 to 60 minutes of wall-clock time per task for 10 to 20 minutes of data, partly because of teleoperator mistakes. Corrections come from the same imperfect hands.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Rollout. One run of the policy on the task, from a start state to an outcome.
  • Intervention (takeover). The moment an operator overrides the policy, plus what they did next.
  • Pre-intervention segment. The steps just before the takeover: the policy heading toward failure.
  • Corrective segment. The operator's actions after the takeover, logged like a demonstration.
  • Threshold. How bad things must look before an operator acts. Every operator has one, written down or not.
  • Hands-off trial. A rollout where nobody may intervene, run only to measure the policy (practitioner session 7).
Part 1 · covers what an intervention is and the two labels it carries

One takeover, two labels

8 min live

Draw a rollout as lanes on one clock, as in practitioner session 1. The policy holds control from the start. An operator watches. At some moment they decide the policy is about to fail, and take over; from then on, the commands come from a person. That moment and the steps either side of it are the most informative few seconds in the whole log.

LiveLabel one: the state3 min▶

The takeover timestamp says "from here, an experienced person did not trust the policy". That is a label on the state, not on the whole episode. It is sharper than a success flag at the end, which only tells you the rollout as a whole went wrong. It is also noisier: it records what the operator believed, not what would have happened. Nobody learns whether the policy would have recovered on its own, because the operator stopped it. Treat the timestamp as a judgement, with the same care practitioner session 3 gives to a success flag.

The hatched stretch before the takeover is where the policy was heading toward trouble. Keep those steps. They are the hard case, and a loader that keeps only the corrective segment throws away the evidence of what the policy did wrong.

LiveLabel two: the action3 min▶

After the takeover, the commands come from a person. Logged at the same rate and in the same action space as the original demonstrations, the corrective segment is a new demonstration that starts from a state the old set never visited. That is why the log is worth so much: it fills the gaps in the training data exactly where the current policy shows them.

Two conditions make it usable. The corrective actions must be the command sent, in the units the controller accepted (practitioner session 5). And the switch from policy to operator must be marked on the clock, so a loader never mixes policy actions and human actions under one label.

Self-studyWhat a takeover row should hold2 min▶
  • Rollout id and policy version, so the correction is tied to the policy that needed it.
  • Takeover time on the episode clock, and the hand-back time if control was returned.
  • Operator id and the threshold policy in force that day (Part 3).
  • Pointers to the pre-intervention segment and the corrective segment, both kept.
  • The outcome after the correction, with its rule.
Part 2 · covers what the intervention log hides

The log records the operator too

12 min live

An intervention only happens where an operator noticed trouble and chose to act. So the log is a sample of the policy's failures filtered through one person's attention and one person's threshold. A cautious operator steps in early: many interventions, almost no failures, and a share of takeovers the policy did not need. A loose operator waits: few interventions, and failures that never reach the log because nobody intervened.

The operator sees risk plus noise (standard deviation 0.12) and takes over when what they see exceeds the threshold. Move the threshold; the policy never changes.

LiveRead the default, then drag5 min▶

At the default threshold of 0.5 the model prints 86 interventions in 200 rollouts, 43.0 per 100. Behind them, 59 rollouts would have failed without help. The operator caught all but 1 of those, and 28 of the 86 takeovers were on rollouts that would have been fine. Now drag the threshold up and down. The 59 never moves, because the policy never moves. The intervention rate swings widely anyway.

That is the whole lesson in one slider: an intervention rate is a joint measurement of the policy and the person watching it. Compare two policies by intervention rate with two different operators, or the same operator on a tired day and a fresh one, and you are partly comparing operators.

LiveThe judgement inside the log4 min▶

Kress-Gazit et al. (2024) ask for "a clear, detailed, and unambiguous definition of success" and recommend that the person who designed the behaviour is not the one who evaluates it. A takeover is a success judgement made in real time, by one person, with no written rule. Zhao et al. (2023) found human demonstrations "inherently stochastic" even from one person; the decision to step in is at least as variable. Write down the threshold policy ("take over when the parcel is within 2 cm of the tote edge"), and expect it to drift anyway.

Real world

In DROID (Khazatsky et al. 2024), collectors marked each of the 76k episodes as success or failure, and that human mark decided what the policy was trained on: failed episodes were excluded, and about 16k unsuccessful trajectories were released separately. The same pattern holds for takeovers: a person's in-the-moment call decides which states become training data.

Self-studyDisengagements: what we link instead of reteach3 min▶

The same trap appears at scale in driving, where a safety driver's takeover is counted as a disengagement and reported per distance driven. How those counts are reported, and why miles between disengagements is a fragile comparison across fleets, is taught in learn autonomous driving. What is particular to a manipulation bench is the scale: tens or hundreds of rollouts, one or two operators, and the corrections fed straight back into the next policy within days.

Part 3 · covers logging, training and evaluation done separately

Two paths out of the rollouts

5 min live

The log has two jobs that pull against each other. As training data it should be rich in hard states, which is exactly what operators produce. As evidence about the policy it is biased by the operator. The fix is structural: corrections go to training, and the policy is judged on separate hands-off trials where nobody may intervene. No rollout id appears on both paths.

LiveFour habits for the log3 min▶
  • Log the operator and the threshold policy with every intervention. Without them, a change in the rate cannot be told apart from a change of person or of rule.
  • Keep the pre-intervention segment. It is the hard case. A loader that keeps only the recovery loses the evidence of the failure.
  • Evaluate on separate, hands-off trials. Paired start states, a written success rule, an interval (practitioner session 7). Never judge a policy by intervention rate alone.
  • Mix corrections into training with care. They over-represent hard states, which is the point. They also over-represent the style of whichever operator intervened most. Know each operator's share before you train.
LiveWhy the trials must be separate2 min▶

Kress-Gazit et al. (2024) recommend evaluating all policies in one session, interleaved and blind to the evaluator, with matched initial conditions. They also show how fragile a success rate is: the same policy, re-run on the same initial conditions after a lab reorganisation, dropped from 13/20 to 0/8. An assisted rollout cannot meet any of these conditions, because the person deciding when to step in is also shaping the outcome. Only a rollout nobody touches measures the policy.

Real world

DROID (Khazatsky et al. 2024) evaluated policies with 10 rollouts per task setting per method, in A/B comparisons: a separate evaluation, not a by-product of data collection.

Build-along

An intervention log in pandas, per operator

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Python 3.10 or later with numpy and pandas. You build a constructed Wrenfield log: three operators with different thresholds, 40 assisted rollouts each, plus 20 hands-off trials. Then you compute the intervention rate and the share of corrective steps per operator, and split corrections for training away from trials for evaluation. The figure draws the per-operator rates the script printed.

Build the constructed log

Three operators watch the same policy. Each rollout has a hidden risk; the operator sees it with noise and takes over above their threshold. A takeover writes a time and a number of corrective steps. Twenty more rollouts are hands-off trials: nobody may intervene.

log.py, part 1import numpy as np import pandas as pd rng = np.random.default_rng(8) # constructed Wrenfield log FAIL = 0.65 # hidden: risk above this fails ops = {"op-03": 0.35, "op-07": 0.50, "op-11": 0.70} rows = [] for op, th in ops.items(): for i in range(40): # 40 assisted rollouts each risk = rng.uniform() seen = risk + rng.normal(0, 0.12) hit = seen > th rows.append({ "rollout_id": f"{op}-{i:02d}", "mode": "assisted", "operator": op, "threshold": th, "intervened": hit, "t_takeover": round(rng.uniform(2, 9), 2) if hit else None, "corrective_steps": int(rng.integers(40, 160)) if hit else 0, "success": bool(hit or risk <= FAIL)}) for i in range(20): # 20 hands-off trials (p7) risk = rng.uniform() rows.append({ "rollout_id": f"trial-{i:02d}", "mode": "trial", "operator": None, "threshold": None, "intervened": False, "t_takeover": None, "corrective_steps": 0, "success": bool(risk <= FAIL)}) log = pd.DataFrame(rows) print(log.head(3).to_string(index=False))

Rate and share, per operator

Group the assisted rollouts by operator. Count interventions and the failures nobody caught, turn interventions into a rate per 100 rollouts, and compute each operator's share of all corrective steps.

log.py, part 2assisted = log[log["mode"] == "assisted"].copy() assisted["failed_alone"] = ~assisted["intervened"] & ~assisted["success"] per_op = assisted.groupby("operator").agg( threshold=("threshold", "first"), rollouts=("rollout_id", "count"), interventions=("intervened", "sum"), failed_alone=("failed_alone", "sum"), corrective_steps=("corrective_steps", "sum")) per_op["rate_per_100"] = 100 * per_op["interventions"] / per_op["rollouts"] per_op["share_of_steps"] = (per_op["corrective_steps"] / per_op["corrective_steps"].sum()).round(2) print(per_op.to_string())

Two paths: training and evaluation

Corrections go to training; hands-off trials go to evaluation. The assert is the wall from Part 3: no rollout id on both sides.

log.py, part 3train = assisted[assisted["intervened"]] # corrections -> training evals = log[log["mode"] == "trial"] # hands-off -> evaluation assert not set(train["rollout_id"]) & set(evals["rollout_id"]) print("training corrections:", len(train), "episodes,", int(train["corrective_steps"].sum()), "steps") print("evaluation trials:", len(evals), "| successes:", int(evals["success"].sum()), "of", len(evals))

Extend it: cap one operator's share

Add a step that samples corrections so no operator supplies more than 40 percent of the corrective steps, and print the new shares. Then ask what you lost: op-03's extra corrections were also the earliest looks at hard states.

The habit to keep. The rate column moved from 72.5 to 35.0 across three operators watching one unchanged policy. The only number in that output that speaks about the policy itself is the last line, the hands-off trials, and practitioner session 7 tells you how little 14 of 20 pins down.

Check yourself

Three questions

An operator takes over a Wrenfield rollout at 6.4 seconds and finishes the pick by hand. Which two labels does that one event carry?

The takeover time marks a state the policy could not handle; the corrective segment is a demonstration starting from that state. Keep both, and keep the steps before the takeover too.

Policy B shows half the intervention rate of policy A. Why is that not enough to say B is better?

An intervention needs someone to notice and choose to act. Change the operator or their threshold and the rate moves while the policy stays still, as the slider showed.

Why must evaluation trials be separate from the assisted rollouts that feed the intervention log?

A rollout the operator rescued cannot say whether the policy would have failed. Hands-off trials, kept off the training path, are the only clean evidence of improvement.

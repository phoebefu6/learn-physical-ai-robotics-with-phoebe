Domain randomization

A simulator is never the real cell. Domain randomization stops trying to make it match and instead makes it vary: random textures, random lights, a camera that never sits in quite the same place, until the real world looks like one more variation the model has already seen. Tonight you read the idea at its source, look at which knobs actually mattered in the original ablation, and then turn two knobs yourself on Wrenfield Lab's grasp bench to find the width where randomizing stops helping and starts hurting. Wrenfield Lab and every one of its trials are constructed; the research numbers are real and cited.

Part 0

Wrenfield's simulator says 100 percent; the real bench says 29 of 50

Wrenfield Lab (constructed) trained its parcel-grasp policy on 400 simulated episodes rendered under one lighting setting and one camera position. In simulation it never missed. On the real packing bench, which is dimmer than the simulator and has its camera mounted a little differently, it succeeded 29 times in 50. That 42-point gap between the simulator and the real cell is the problem domain randomization was invented for. The fix is not a better simulator. It is a simulator that refuses to be consistent.

LiveWhy randomize instead of match3 min▶

The obvious way to close a sim-to-real gap is to make the simulator more realistic: measure the real lights, photograph the real textures, model the real lens. Every one of those is expensive, and every one is still wrong in some detail the model will find. Domain randomization takes the opposite bet. If the training data contains enough deliberately unrealistic variation, the model has to learn the features that survive all of it, and the real scene becomes just another draw.

Real world

Tobin, Fong, Ray, Schneider, Zaremba and Abbeel (2017) trained an object detector only on simulated images with non-realistic random textures. Without a single real image in training, it located real objects to within 1.5 cm on average, stayed robust to distractors and partial occlusion, and let a robot detect and pick the target in 38 of 40 trials in cluttered scenes.

Self-studyWords for tonight3 min▶
  • Domain randomization. Varying the simulator's appearance and setup at random in every training episode, so the model cannot rely on any one rendering.
  • Width. How far a randomized setting may move from its centre: "lighting plus or minus 0.4", "camera plus or minus 3 cm". A width is a hyperparameter, chosen and recorded like a learning rate.
  • Distractor. An object in the scene that is not the target. Randomizing their number and shape teaches the model what to ignore.
  • Sim-to-real gap. Success in simulation minus success on the real robot, in percentage points.
  • Ablation. Removing one ingredient at a time and measuring what it was worth.
  • Uniform randomization. Drawing every setting with equal probability across its width, the default in most pipelines.
Part 1 · covers the idea at source: what was randomized and what it bought

Make the simulator vary until reality is one more sample

7 min live

Read the 2017 paper the way a data team reads a pipeline spec: which columns vary, over what range, and what the output was. Tobin et al. did not randomize one thing. They randomized nearly everything that a camera image depends on, and each knob had a declared range. The figure lays the knobs out on the left and the result on the right: a cloud of scenes, none of them realistic, with the real scene sitting somewhere inside it.

LiveReading the knobs like a data spec4 min▶

Every knob has a range, and the range is a decision. The camera was not placed anywhere at all. Each sample sat inside a 10 by 5 by 10 cm box around a hand-placed starting point that roughly matched the real camera, with its viewing angle offset by up to 0.1 rad and its field of view scaled by up to 5 percent. That is a width centred on a guess about reality, not a width centred on nothing.

The textures were deliberately unrealistic. Random textures on every object, the table, the floor, the skybox and the robot itself. The model cannot learn "the target is the red one" because red means nothing from one image to the next; it has to learn shape and position.

Clutter was part of the training set. Between 0 and 10 distractor objects per scene. Part 2 shows this turned out to be the most important single knob.

Then they measured on the real robot. 1.5 cm average localization error, and 38 of 40 successful grasps in cluttered scenes. In practitioner session 7 terms: 38 of 40 is a rate with a trial count, and it should travel with its interval.

Self-studyWhat the result does not show4 min▶

The gap did not close. In simulation the detector's error was 0.3 to 0.5 cm; on real images it was 1.5 cm. The authors state that the model still over-fits to simulation. Randomization shrinks a sim-to-real gap; it does not promise to remove it.

There was nothing real to validate on. With no real data at training time, the authors note that "no validation on real data can be done during training". Every choice of width was checked only after the fact, on the robot. That is the practical cost of the method: your tuning loop runs through the real cell.

Data volume mattered, up to a point. The detector was relatively accurate with as few as 5,000 training samples (starting from pre-trained weights) and kept improving up to about 50,000. With 10,000 images, performance degraded significantly when fewer than 1,000 unique textures were used. Variety is a resource you can run short of, like rows.

Part 2 · covers the ablation: which knobs mattered

Not every knob is worth the same

5 min live

Randomizing everything is easy to say and expensive to build. An ablation tells you where to spend. Tobin et al. retrained with one ingredient removed at a time and measured real detection error under three conditions: the object alone, the object among distractors, and the object partly hidden. Lower is better.

LiveReading Table II as a priority list3 min▶

Distractors: critical, in the authors' word. Without them, error with distractors present jumps from 1.8 to 7.2 cm, and with occlusions from 2.4 to 7.4 cm. On a clean table you would never have noticed: 1.3 against 1.5.

Camera: a slight boost, consistently. Dropping camera randomization raises error under all three conditions, from 1.3 to 2.0, 1.8 to 2.4 and 2.4 to 2.9 cm. Small, but present everywhere.

Noise: negligible. 1.4, 1.9 and 2.4 cm against the full method's 1.3, 1.8 and 2.4. If budget is short, this is the knob to build last.

The data-team habit. Report ablations per condition, never as one averaged error. The averaged number would have hidden that one knob carried the whole cluttered-scene result.

Self-studyWidths are hyperparameters, so log them like ones4 min▶

Each randomized setting carries a range, a sampling distribution and a centre. Those three numbers decide what the training data contains, which makes them part of the dataset's identity exactly as calibration was in practitioner session 2. A policy trained at "camera plus or minus 3 cm" and one trained at "plus or minus 12 cm" are trained on different datasets even if every other line of code is the same.

Practical consequences: store the widths with every simulated episode or every generated batch, sweep them the way you sweep a learning rate, and evaluate the sweep on the real cell with trial counts and intervals. Tobin et al.'s texture finding (performance degraded below 1,000 unique textures at 10,000 images) is a reminder that "how many distinct values did the randomizer actually produce" is itself a column worth logging.

Wider helps until it does not

LiveReading the width grid4 min▶

Start at the defaults. Lighting 0.4, camera 3 cm: 46 of 50 on the real cell, simulator 98.6 percent, a gap of 6.6 points. With nothing randomized it was 29 of 50 and a gap of 42 points.

The camera knob is an on-off switch here. Along the 0.4 row, camera widths of 1.5, 3, 6 and 12 cm all give 46 of 50. In this constructed world the camera's offset moves the parcel and the table fiducial by the same amount, a straight-line effect, so the moment the simulator varies the camera at all, the policy learns to subtract the fiducial. Varying it more teaches nothing new and, here, costs nothing either.

The lighting knob has a sweet spot. With the camera on, lighting 0.2 and 0.4 both give 46; 0.7 gives 44 while the simulator itself falls to about 76 percent; 0.95 gives 19 of 50, with the simulator at about 48 percent. The harm comes from the lighting width, because the lighting effect is curved and the policy is a straight line (the figure above). Spread the training over almost-black to almost-double and the line is fitted to conditions the real cell never sees.

Read the breakdown at 0.95. The scorecard shows where the misses live: ordinary light is the condition that collapses. The policy got worse precisely at the conditions the real cell is made of.

Self-studyThe disagreement: how wide is wide enough5 min▶

Mehta, Diaz, Golemo, Pal and Paull (2019) argue that domain randomization "may lead to suboptimal, high-variance policies", and attribute it to sampling environment parameters uniformly. Their answer is to choose which settings to sample, actively, rather than spreading training evenly across a wide range. Tobin et al. (2017) found that more variety helped, up to a point, in textures and training images. Both can be true, and the literature has not settled a general rule for how wide a width should be.

The working norm that follows: width is a per-robot judgement, made by sweeping and checked on the real cell, with trial counts and intervals next to every rate. A wide setting that looks safe in a design review can cost real accuracy; a narrow one can leave the real cell outside the cloud. Only the real trials decide.

One honest oddity in the constructed grid: at lighting 0.95, turning the camera width on drops the score from 25 to 19 of 50, the opposite of every other row. Fifty trials and a straight-line policy have quirks, and so will yours. Treat a single surprising cell as a reason to run more trials, not as a law.

Build-along

Sweep the widths and keep a lab notebook

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No Python tonight: the widget in Part 3 is your training run. You will find the smallest widths that reach the best score, over-randomize until it breaks, and compare the best randomization can do with what twenty real demonstrations do on the bench. Each step ends with a lab notebook line; the boxes hold the canon values for seed 1122, so you can check yours. The figure is the whole grid you are about to walk.

Turn both on at their smallest nonzero settings. Then check that the defaults (0.4 and 3 cm) give the same real score. Smaller widths mean less simulation spent on conditions far from the real cell.

Widen the camera alone

Keep lighting at 0.4 and step the camera through 1.5, 3, 6 and 12 cm. Write down what changes and give the reason in one sentence.

Notebook line 4Lighting 0.4, camera 1.5 / 3 / 6 / 12 cm: 46 of 50 each time. A straight-line camera effect is learned as soon as it varies; more width adds nothing here.

Over-randomize the lighting and record where it breaks

Keep the camera at 3 cm and step lighting to 0.7, then 0.95. Watch both the real score and the simulator's own score. At 0.95, read which condition collapsed in the scorecard.

Notebook line 5Lighting 0.7: 44 of 50, simulator about 76%. Lighting 0.95: 19 of 50, simulator about 48%, worse than no randomization (29). Breaks between 0.4 and 0.7; collapses by 0.95.

Compare the best width with real demonstrations

Open the bench in practitioner session 10 and read rung 4: the same 0.4 and 3 cm widths plus twenty real demonstrations. Write the comparison and what it says about the limit of randomization.

Notebook line 6Best randomization alone: 46 of 50, with 17 of 21 at the bench edges. Plus 20 real demos (bench rung 4): 49 of 50. No width teaches the real lens; the simulator does not contain it.

The habit to keep. A width sweep is an experiment with a notebook: the widths, the seed, real successes over trials, the simulator's score, and the condition that moved. A config file that says "lighting 0.95" with no record of why is how over-randomization ships.

Check yourself

Three questions

In Tobin et al.'s ablation (2017), which removed ingredient hurt most when distractors were present in the real scene?

Against the full method's 1.8 cm, dropping distractors from training raised error to 7.2 cm. On a clean table the same model was barely worse (1.5 against 1.3), which is why per-condition reporting matters.

On Wrenfield's constructed bench, camera widths of 1.5, 3, 6 and 12 cm all give 46 of 50 at lighting 0.4. Why does widening the camera beyond any nonzero value stop mattering here?

Once the simulator varies the camera at all, the regression sees the two readings move together and learns to cancel the offset. Width 0 gives 37 at lighting 0.4 because the policy never learned to subtract.

At lighting 0.95 the simulator's own success falls to about 48 percent. Why does over-randomization lower simulator success too, not only real success?

The seed and episode count are fixed; only the widths change. A limited policy spread over a huge range spends its accuracy on the extremes and misses the middle, in simulation and on the real cell alike.
